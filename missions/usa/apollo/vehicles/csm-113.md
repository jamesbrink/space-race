# CSM-113 · Casper

Ken Mattingly flew Casper in lunar orbit while John Young and Charlie Duke explored the Descartes highlands. Young occupied the left couch at launch, Mattingly the centre and Duke the right. The gallery includes all three stations, a through-hatch, the common Block II docking probe and navigation optics. The crew returned both orbital-camera film cassettes in the command module. [Apollo 16 Mission Report, MSC-07230, §§3, 5 and 9.12.1](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19720026127)

## A yaw drive that held up the landing

During a checkout after undocking, Mattingly saw the service engine oscillate when he selected the secondary yaw servo. The primary servo was healthy. The oscillation of **2.4 Hz** is **documented**. The two spacecraft flew close together while controllers checked whether the backup would be safe to use under thrust. The crew received clearance to proceed, and Casper later used the engine to leave lunar orbit. Postflight investigation established an open rate-feedback loop. The returned command-module electronics and wiring passed their checks. Either a connector fault or a broken wire in the discarded service module could explain the fault. [MSC-07230, §§6.6 and 14.1.10, figs. 14-10–14-12](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19720026127)

The separate yaw-drive view exposes the finned housing, harness plug, cable run and lower clamp beside the actuator shaft. Figure 14-12 shows the harness under strain at that clamp as the actuator moves. The exhibit uses the common engine’s axial geometry and estimates the **6¼ × 6 × 7 in** housing and clamp placement from the photograph. [MSC-07230, fig. 14-12](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19720026127) · [Shared propulsion housing](service-tanks.md)

## Orbital instruments and film return

The sector I panel separated at **69:59:01** elapsed. The crew saw the panel tumble away. Behind it, the mapping camera, stellar camera and laser altimeter shared the upper shelf. The panoramic camera and removable film cassette were below. The mapping and panoramic cameras had **3-inch** and **24-inch** focal lengths respectively, both **documented**. The crew's spacewalk showed the stellar glare shield caught against a handrail. Mattingly retrieved both cassettes and inspected the guide pins on the gamma-ray head. The other boom, which carried the mass spectrometer, had stalled during retraction and was jettisoned before transearth injection. [MSC-07230, table 3-I, §§5.1, 5.4, 9.12.1 and 14.5.2, table A-III](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19720026127)

Appendix A.1 records the two proximity switches added to the boom installation to indicate position during extension and retraction. The model shows the switch housings, leads, guide shoes and pins with both heads in launch stowage. The gamma-ray instrument and fixed X-ray and alpha-particle instruments are distinct from the mass spectrometer. [MSC-07230, §§5.1–5.4 and appendix A.1](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19720026127)

The hexagonal PFS-2 subsatellite measured **30 inches long and 14 inches across** and weighed **90 lb**, as **documented** in the press kit. Its three folded booms extended to five feet following ejection. The orbit-shaping burn was omitted after the yaw-drive anomaly. From its orbit and final loss of signal, investigators calculated that PFS-2 struck the far side on 29 May 1972 during its 425th revolution. The dedicated Gallery view reveals the stowed satellite inside its launch case. [Apollo 16 Press Kit, “Subsatellite”](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/static/apollo50th/pdf/A16_PressKit.pdf) · [MSC-07230, §5.5](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19720026127)

## Power and consumables

Sector I also held the third oxygen and hydrogen tanks. Sector IV housed the first two of each, three fuel cells and the auxiliary battery. The flight report describes Teflon separators between battery cases and end shells, revised vent settings and strengthened plate tabs. The electrical cutaway retains the preceding ship’s valve arrangement and oxygen heater reconstruction. Its **26.55-inch oxygen** and **31.80-inch hydrogen** dewar diameters, **415 Ah** auxiliary battery capacity and **16.94 × 9.04 × 9.96-inch** battery envelope are **assumed** from the sister installation. [Apollo 16 Press Kit, “Command and Service Modules”](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/static/apollo50th/pdf/A16_PressKit.pdf) · [MSC-07230, appendix A.1–A.2](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19720026127) · [CSM-112 hardware](csm-112.md)

## Reconstruction limits

- The flight report identifies CSM-112 as the reference installation. The **404.25-inch** service-nozzle-to-docking-plane span, pressure-vessel contours, removable hatch, **10-inch** active probe stroke and flight instruments follow the documented common hardware and sister ships. Console switches and markings approximate the CM-106 layout; timer fuses, eyepiece heater, water-dispenser insert, clear instrument guards and additional separation breakers are internal or omitted. [Apollo CSM](apollo-csm.md) · [MSC-07230, appendix A.1](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19720026127)
- The yaw housing, harness and boom switch locations are **estimated**. The drive stays at its neutral attitude. Its motor internals, the failed electrical connection, the boom drives’ internal sensors and the chemistry inside the auxiliary battery are omitted.
- The SIM bay follows the flight’s instrument arrangement. Local shelf positions, finishes and closures are **estimated** from the shared J-mission reconstruction. The Gallery displaces the door four metres to expose the instruments. The cameras and booms remain stowed, with optical ports exposed for inspection. Individual covers, the jammed glare shield, cassette retrieval, deployed subsatellite and orbital trajectory are omitted. [MSC-07230, appendix A.4](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19720026127) · [Apollo 16 Preliminary Science Report, NASA SP-315, fig. 2-4](https://www.lpi.usra.edu/lunar/documents/NASA%20SP%20315.pdf)
