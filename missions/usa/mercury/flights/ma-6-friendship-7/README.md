# Mercury-Atlas 6, *Friendship 7*

The first American in orbit: three times round the Earth, most of it flown by hand, and down
through the atmosphere with the retropack still strapped across the heat shield.

## What happened

Two Americans had gone up and come straight down. Two Russians had been round the world, one of
them seventeen times. Glenn's flight, first meant for 23 January, slipped from day to day for the
weather; on the 27th he lay in the capsule for five hours before cloud ended the count; then fuel
was found between two bulkheads of the Atlas, which took days to put right; then the weather
again, three counts begun and stopped.

On 20 February he was woken at 2:20 and was in the capsule a little after six. A guidance unit in
the Atlas had been changed while he waited in the van. As the hatch went on, one of its seventy
bolts was found broken. Grissom had flown with one missing; this time the hatch came off again and
forty minutes went. A valve stuck. A tracking station lost its power. At 09:47 the Atlas left.

"It's a little bumpy about here," he said, where the air's pressure was greatest. The booster engines went at two
minutes; the tower at two and a half; at five minutes the sustainer stopped, three small rockets
pushed the capsule off, and it turned itself round so that he rode backwards, looking at where he
had been. The empty Atlas was tumbling a hundred yards behind him. He was told he had speed enough
for seven orbits at least.

Over the Indian Ocean he saw his first sunset from orbit; over Australia, at night, the lights of
Perth, left on for him. At sunrise over the Pacific the capsule was suddenly in a cloud of small
bright specks, drifting slowly past the window. He called them fireflies. They came again at each
sunrise, and nobody that day could say what they were.

Near the end of the first orbit the capsule began to drift to the right, a degree and a half a
second, like a car with its wheels out of line. The autopilot called for the small jet that
should have stopped it and nothing came. Glenn took the capsule in hand, tried each way of
steering it, and settled on fly-by-wire as the cheapest in fuel. Twenty minutes later the jet
came back, and the one opposite it stopped. He flew most of the next two orbits himself; much of
what he had been sent to observe was dropped.

At the same time a signal reached the Cape, on a channel called Segment 51, that said the heat
shield was no longer latched to the capsule. The shield is unlatched on purpose just before
landing, to drop four feet and pull out a cushion of air. If it had let go in orbit, the only
thing holding it on was the three straps of the retropack. The controllers told the tracking
stations to ask him, each in turn, whether his landing-bag switch was off; he guessed what they
were not saying. They decided that after the retrorockets had fired the spent pack should be kept
on instead of dropped, so that its straps would hold the shield until the air did.

The retrorockets fired over California at 04:33:08, five seconds apart: "feels like I'm going
halfway back to Hawaii". Keeping the pack on meant drawing the periscope in by hand and overriding
the switch that senses the first of the air. Then there was a bump, and he thought the pack had
gone. A strap swung round in front of the window. Burning pieces went past: "a real fireball
outside". He did not know whether they were the pack or the shield. Radio was lost for some
minutes, as expected. When it came back he said his condition was good, "but that was a real
fireball, boy".

Both fuel tanks ran dry on the way down and the capsule swung like a falling leaf. He reached for
the switch to let the drogue parachute out early; it came out by itself as he did. The main
parachute followed, the heat shield dropped on its bag as it should, and at 04:55:23 the capsule
was in the Atlantic, forty miles short, because nobody had allowed for the fuel and oxygen it had
used up. The destroyer *Noa* was six miles away. She had him on deck inside twenty minutes, still
in the capsule; it was too hot to climb out through the top, so he told the sailors to stand clear
and blew the hatch. The plunger kicked back and cut his knuckles through the glove. It was his
only injury.

The signal had come from a faulty switch. The shield had been latched all the time.

The morning, by the clocks at the Cape (the same report, Table 8-II and p. 67; *This New Ocean*,
pp. 422 to 426): woken 2:20; suiting from 4:28; into the van 5:05; at the pad 5:20; a guidance unit
in the Atlas changed from 5:25, which cost 135 minutes in all; up the gantry at 5:58 as the sky
began to clear; into the spacecraft 6:06 (the history has 6:03); the broken hatch bolt found at
7:10, about 40 minutes; the count on again at 8:05 from T-60; a hold to add ten gallons of
propellant; a fuel pump outlet valve stuck at 8:58, T-22, until 9:25; Bermuda's power failed at
T-6½ minutes. In all 2 hours 17 minutes of holds, and liftoff 3 hours 44 minutes after he went
aboard. The liquid oxygen went aboard from about T-35 minutes; the umbilical was thrown off at
T-35 seconds; the engines were started at T-0 and the vehicle left about four seconds later (the
report does not say which of the two its 14:47:39 is).

All five planets that the eye can see stood within thirty degrees of the Sun that morning, in the
same part of the sky. None could be seen by day. Uranus was below the horizon and Neptune low in the
west.

**The weather.** No figures have been read. The history has a "cloudy February morning", overcast
until about 5:58, "when the sky began to clear", and Glenn at the periscope after 7:10: "Looks like
the weather is breaking up" (*This New Ocean*, pp. 422 and 423). The photographs of the liftoff
show a blue sky. In the landing area: three-foot waves, a tenth of cloud, ten miles' visibility
(the transcript, 04:42:27); the air 76 °F (*Results*, p. 34). The hourly observations of the Cape's
weather station have not been looked up, as they were for Liberty Bell 7.

## What is not built

- **The launch site.** Complex 14 is [a site as numbers](../../sites/lc-14.ts): where it was, and
  which way the vehicle faced on it. Its stand, ramp, umbilical tower and service structure are
  drawn as scenery from Complex 5, 5.8 km away (`sites/cape.md`), and are not a place to stand in.
- **The pilot, standing**, and **a cabin to sit in**: the capsule's inside is modelled and cannot
  be boarded. The figure on the couch is the suit as modelled for Shepard with Glenn's name on it
  ([spacecraft.md](spacecraft.md), "The pilot").
- **The flight.** Nothing here can be flown. It wants the Atlas's weights, thrust and pitch
  programme, which have not been read (the launch vehicle's own report for 109-D, if it survives);
  an orbit and an entry as data (`Flight.orbital`, as Vostok 1 has); the control modes, with a jet
  that fails; the retropack kept on, and burning; and a landing forty miles short for the reason it
  was.
- **The periscope is not seen to move.** Its door, objective and the link between them are built as
  one linkage and carry their motion, and `flight.ts` has when each went on this flight (out at
  separation; in by hand at 04:40:30; "Scope did not come out" under the drogue, and pumped out by
  hand from 04:49:34: *Results*, pp. 71, 133, 150, 189 and 191). With no clock in the gallery
  nothing plays them: the door is shut and the objective in, as at liftoff.

## Where sources disagree

- **How long the *Noa* took.** *Results* (p. 81) has the capsule aboard 20 minutes after landing;
  NASA SP-45 (p. 12) "approximately 17 minutes"; *This New Ocean* (p. 433) has her alongside after
  17. The programme's README here said 21, from memory; nothing read says so.
- **When he went aboard.** 6:06 in the report's table; 6:03 in the control centre's log, as the
  history quotes it.
- **When the count was stopped on 27 January.** T-13 minutes (*Results*, p. 67) or T-20 (*This New
  Ocean*).
- **The engines' thrust.** NASA SP-45 gives 154,000 lbf for each booster engine and 57,000 for the
  sustainer; the press kit for MA-9, 150,000 and 60,000; *Results* (p. 45) "approximately 360,000
  pounds" in all. The model's data carries SP-45's ratings and says they are ratings.
- **The landing point.** Reported where the capsule was picked up, 21°25.6′ N 68°36.5′ W; predicted
  21°31.2′ N 68°52.9′ W (NASA SP-45, Table 8-III). The landing itself was not fixed more closely.
- **The launch azimuth.** *Results* (p. 46) gives a heading "about 75 degrees" at insertion and the
  pad's pitch axis on 105°; *This New Ocean* gives 72.51° for MA-5 and says MA-6 was to be the same.
- **The panel.** The report's figure of this capsule's panel (Figure 2-4) is the drawing printed
  for Liberty Bell 7's. See [spacecraft.md](spacecraft.md), "Cabin".

## Where the spacecraft is now

At the Smithsonian's National Air and Space Museum in Washington (object A19670176000: 190.5 cm
across and 226.1 cm tall as kept, 875 kg), with its hatch off and its paint burned thin.
The traces of the name under the window, of the flag and of UNITED STATES are where the pad
photographs have them.
