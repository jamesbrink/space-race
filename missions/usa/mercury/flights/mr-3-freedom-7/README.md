# Mercury-Redstone 3, *Freedom 7*

The first American in space, and the first person to control a spacecraft's attitude by hand.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Launch | 5 May 1961, 14:34:13 UTC (09:34 EST) |
| Site | Cape Canaveral, Launch Complex 5 |
| Pilot | Alan B. Shepard Jr. (backup: John Glenn) |
| Spacecraft | Mercury No. 7, McDonnell Aircraft: [spacecraft.md](spacecraft.md) |
| Launch vehicle | Mercury-Redstone MRLV, MR-7 |
| Trajectory | suborbital; apogee 187.5 km (101.2 nmi), range 487.3 km (263.1 nmi) |
| Peak velocity | about 8,340 km/h (5,180 mph) |
| Loads | 6.3 g at booster cutoff, 11.6 g on entry |
| Duration | 15 min 22 s |
| Splashdown | 27°14′N 75°53′W, North Atlantic |
| Recovery | helicopter to USS *Lake Champlain*, 11 minutes after splashdown |
| Mass | 1,830 kg (4,040 lb) at launch; 1,051 kg (2,316 lb) at landing |

## What happened

Vostok 1 had put Yuri Gagarin in orbit three weeks earlier. Shepard's flight was shorter and never
reached orbit, but it did something Gagarin's had not: the pilot flew the ship. After separating
from the Redstone, Freedom 7 turned heat-shield-forward on its automatic system, and Shepard then
took over one axis at a time (pitch, then yaw, then roll) until he held all three by hand. He
looked at the ground through the periscope, fired the three retrorockets as a test of the system
the orbital flights would depend on, and rode the capsule through an 11.6 g entry to a landing
within sight of the recovery force.

The count had held for hours; Shepard had been in the capsule over four hours at liftoff.

## Where the spacecraft is now

Freedom 7 is on display at the John F. Kennedy Presidential Library and Museum in Boston, on loan
from the Smithsonian's National Air and Space Museum.
