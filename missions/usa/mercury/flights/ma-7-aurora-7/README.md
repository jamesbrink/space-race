# Mercury-Atlas 7, *Aurora 7*

The second American in orbit, and the first sent up with experiments of his own to do: three times
round the Earth, a horizon scanner that misled the autopilot from the launch on, and a landing 250
nautical miles beyond the ships waiting for it.

pad can be turned, taken apart as they staged, and cut open. Its launch site and the flight itself
are not built: see "What is not built" below.

## What happened

Glenn had shown that a man could fly the capsule. Carpenter, who had been his backup and had spent
more hours in Friendship 7 than he would in his own, was given a flight plan with science in it: a
balloon to tow, a ball of liquid to watch, the horizon to photograph through filters, flares to
look for over Australia, the band of light Glenn had seen above the horizon at night to measure.

He was woken at 1:15 and was in the capsule by a quarter to five. All seventy bolts of the hatch
went in. Fog and smoke lay on the Cape, and the cameras that would have to watch an abort could
not see; the count was held at T-11 minutes for forty-five minutes in all. At 07:45 EST
the Atlas left, in a mile's visibility under broken cloud at 700 ft.

The launch was the quietest so far. The capsule left the Atlas at 5 minutes 12 seconds; Carpenter
turned it round by hand, for a third of the fuel the autopilot had used for Glenn, and was told he
had speed for seven orbits. What nobody knew was that the pitch horizon scanner had been reading
some 18° wrong since the launch, and was steering the gyros with it.

He photographed the horizon, the spent Atlas with its trail of ice, the Sun going down. The
balloon went out at 1 h 38 min: a spray of foil confetti, a block of balsa he took for the balloon,
and then the thing itself, half filled and wrinkled, wandering at the end of its line. It would
not be cut loose. The green liquid climbed into its tube as it was meant to. The suit ran hot, and
he was a long time finding a valve setting that cooled it. He manoeuvred a great deal, more than
once with two sets of jets working against his hand at the same time, and by the end of the second
orbit both fuel tanks were under half. He drifted through most of the third to save what was left.
At dawn he reached for an instrument, knocked the hatch, and a cloud of Glenn's "fireflies" went
past the window; he knocked again and made more.

Over Hawaii, minutes before the retrofire, the horizon in the window and the needles on the
panel did not agree. He was behind on his checklist. The capsule's clock would fire the rockets
only if the gyros said the attitude was right, and they said it was not, so he bypassed it, held
the capsule on the window and the periscope, and pushed the button himself. The rockets lit three
seconds late, with the capsule yawed 25° to one side, and thrust a little under their rating. The
ground knew at once that he would land long.

The manual tank was already empty. The automatic one ran dry in the entry, the capsule began to
swing, and at 25,000 ft, with the Sun crossing the window through 45°, he fired the drogue by
hand. The main parachute did not come out by itself at 10,600 ft: a barostat added five days
before, to keep it from coming out in orbit, was reading the cabin's pressure. He pulled that
too. "Your landing point is 200 miles long," the Cape told him. "We will jump the air rescue
people to you."

Aurora 7 came down 250 nautical miles past the nearest ship, floating low and leaning hard. He
would not blow the hatch at the waterline. He climbed out through the nose, turned his raft over,
and sat in it. An aeroplane found him in 36 minutes; two Air Force swimmers jumped to him after an
hour; a Navy helicopter lifted him off after three, by way of one more ducking. For some of that
time the country did not know whether he was alive.

The morning, by the clocks at the Cape (*This New Ocean*, pp. 446 and 447; NASA TN D-3814,
pp. 16 and 17): woken 1:15; suited by 3:25; into the van 3:45; up the gantry at 4:35; into the
spacecraft at T-135 minutes; the hatch going on from T-94 and bolted at T-74; its shingles on by
T-64; the service tower away between T-47 and T-33; liquid oxygen from T-32; held at T-11 for 15,
15, 10 and 5 minutes for the weather and the range's refraction measurements. The pad's cameras
show the umbilical thrown off, the periscope drawing in and its door shutting, in that order
(p. 18); the report does not say when in the count. The engines burned for 2.95 seconds before the
vehicle was let go (p. 98).

The Moon was up in daylight, in the south-west, where the Atlas did not go. Uranus and Neptune
were below the horizon.

**The weather** (NASA TN D-3814, p. 17): at launch, cloud broken at 700 ft, wind 8 knots from
240°, visibility 1 mile in ground fog and smoke, 77 °F, dewpoint 73 °F, humidity 88 per cent.
"Clear blue sky," Carpenter said at 32 seconds, from above it. In the planned landing area at noon
(USS *John R. Pierce*): cloud scattered at 1,000 ft, wind 11 knots from 096°, visibility 10 miles,
waves 3 ft, the air 84 °F. No `weather.ts` is written for this flight: it has no site model for a
sky to stand over.

## What is not built

- **The launch site.** Complex 14 is [a site as numbers](../../sites/lc-14.ts): where it was, and
  which way the vehicle faced on it.
- **The pilot, standing**, and **a cabin to sit in**: the capsule's inside is modelled and cannot
  be boarded. The figure on the couch is the suit as modelled for Shepard with Carpenter's name on
  it ([spacecraft.md](spacecraft.md), "The pilot").
- **The flight.** Nothing here can be flown. It wants the Atlas's weights, thrust and pitch
  programme, which have not been read; an orbit and an entry as data; a horizon scanner that is
  20° wrong and a pilot who finds out; two fuel tanks that can be emptied; a retrofire by hand.
  The numbers a simulator would start from are here: weights and moments of inertia for five
  phases of the flight are in NASA TN D-3814, p. 7.
- **The balloon is not seen to go, nor the liquid to climb.** The balloon is in its can under the
  flap, as at liftoff; the zero-gravity experiment's liquid lies where 1 g puts it.
- **The periscope is not seen to move.** Its door, objective and the link between them carry their
  motion, and `flight.ts` has when each went on this flight; with no clock in the gallery nothing
  plays them.

## Where sources disagree

- **When the retrorockets lit.** NASA TN D-3814's table of events (p. 122) has the first at
  04:33:10.3; its text (p. 61) has the manual circuit closed at 04:33:08 and thrust on at
  04:33:08.5, against 04:33:05 had the clock fired them; its summary and NASA SP-6 say "about 3
  seconds late". `flight.ts` carries the table's times.
- **How high the main parachute came out.** 8,950 ft (TN D-3814, p. 67); "about 9,500 feet"
  (SP-6, p. 4; *This New Ocean*, p. 455); the pilot called it at "Mark 10".
- **Where the balloon was.** "Installed in the recovery compartment" (TN D-3814, pp. 5 and 32);
  "in a cylindrical container located in the antenna canister under the destabilizer flap" (the
  same report, p. 71); "housed ... in the antenna canister" (*This New Ocean*, p. 444; SP-6,
  p. 12). The model follows the fuller description.
- **What it weighed.** The balloon and its bottle about half a pound and the whole installation
  about 5 lb (TN D-3814, p. 71); "the whole balloon package weighed two pounds" (*This New Ocean*).
- **The apogee.** 144.96 nautical miles (TN D-3814, Table XVII); "145" (NASA SP-45, p. 13).
- **The landing point.** 19°27′ N 63°59′ W by integration of the tracking; 19°30′ N 64°15′ W as
  the recovery ship reported the pickup (TN D-3814, pp. 124 and 128).
- **The beacon's range.** The Super SARAH beacon was heard at 250 miles in TN D-3814 (p. 59) and
  *This New Ocean*; NASA SP-6 (p. 7) prints 950.
- **The insulation bulkhead.** NASA SP-45's chapter on the launch vehicle lists its removal among
  the changes made for Mercury (pp. 92 to 95); the same book's history (pp. 12 and 13) and TN
  D-3814 (p. 8) have it cut out of Atlas 109-D on the pad, retained in 107-D, and removed at the
  factory from 113-D on.

## Where the spacecraft is now

At the Museum of Science and Industry in Chicago (Commons: *Aurora 7 the Museum of Science and
Industry in Chicago.JPG*). It has not been read against the model.
