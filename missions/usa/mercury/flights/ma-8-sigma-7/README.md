# Mercury-Atlas 8, *Sigma 7*

The third American in orbit, and the flight that showed the capsule could be flown for a day: six
times round the Earth with most of the fuel still in the tanks, the rockets fired by the clock, and
a landing in the Pacific within sight of the carrier sent to fetch it.

**In this repo: the vehicle, in the gallery.** Spacecraft No. 16 on Atlas 113-D as they left the
pad can be turned, taken apart as they staged, and cut open. Its launch site and the flight itself
are not built: see "What is not built" below.

## What happened

Carpenter had come down 250 miles long with his tanks dry. The flight after his was to double the
time aloft, and its pilot, who had been Carpenter's backup, made it an engineer's flight: learn
what the capsule uses when it is flown with care. He named it for the engineer's sign for a sum.

He was woken at 1:40, ate a bluefish he had speared the day before, and slid into the capsule at
4:41. An ignition key hung from the hand controller's safety pin; there was a steak sandwich in the
glove compartment. The count was held once, for fifteen minutes at T-45, while a radar in the
Canary Islands was mended.

At 07:15:11 EST the Atlas went, and for the first time was not held down to be listened to: its
booster engines had new injectors that did not need it. As it cleared the stand it rolled
clockwise, at a rate that came within a fifth of the figure at which the abort system would have
fired the escape tower, and then steadied. It flew a little high, dropped its booster engines two
seconds early and burned ten seconds long. "You have a go, seven orbit capability."

Schirra turned the capsule round by the instruments, slowly, without looking out, for three tenths
of a pound of fuel, a tenth of what the autopilot spends on the same turn. Then he looked, found the
spent Atlas in the window where it should be, and followed it down the glass with the smallest
jets: "nearly black", with a white band of frost.

One thing went wrong. His suit grew warmer through the first orbit, as Glenn's and Carpenter's
had, and the flight controllers considered bringing him down after one. He had said beforehand how
he would deal with it, half a mark of the cooling valve every ten minutes so as not to freeze it,
and did that; near the mark of 8 the temperature turned. Mercury Control asked him to try 3
instead, it climbed again, and he went back. After two hours he was cool, and said so.

The rest was what he had planned. He let the capsule drift for two and a half hours in all, an
hour and forty-two minutes of it in one stretch with the autopilot switched off, and found it no
trouble to lie at any angle to the Earth. He lined it up in yaw by the window and
by the periscope, by day and by night, and preferred the window. He took a few photographs with a
Hasselblad, ate, looked for flares at Woomera and a searchlight at Durban and saw lightning and
cloud. Over the Indian Ocean the Moon and Venus stood together and he set the capsule by them.
On the night side he saw a band of light above the horizon thick enough to have stars under it.
He knocked on the hatch at sunrise, as Carpenter had, and Glenn's "fireflies" streamed off it.

The clock fired the retrorockets over the Pacific at 8 hours 52 minutes, with the autopilot
holding the capsule: it "did not seem to vary as much as half a degree". He flew the entry on the
rate system, watched it drink the manual tank, saw the cylinder glow green, fired the drogue
himself at 40,000 ft, and came down under "a beautiful chute" 9,000 yards from USS *Kearsarge*,
whose crew had watched him fall. "I want to stay aboard." Swimmers put a collar on the capsule,
a whaleboat brought a line, and forty minutes after landing it stood on the deck. Five minutes
later he blew the hatch, and climbed out with a cut hand.

The morning, by the clocks at the Cape (*This New Ocean*, pp. 471 and 472; NASA TN D-4807,
pp. 25 and 26): woken 1:40; out of Hangar S a little past 4; into the spacecraft at 4:41, T-140
minutes; the hatch going on from T-108 and secure at T-96; its shingles on by T-88; the service
tower away from T-64; held at T-45 for 15 minutes for the Canary Islands radar; liquid oxygen from
T-38. Two cameras at the pad show the umbilical thrown off, the periscope drawing in and its door
shutting, in that order (p. 28); the report does not say when in the count.

launch instant, 12:15:11 UTC. All `derived`. Elevations are geometric.

| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sun | 101.2° | 12.0° | Just south of east, under an hour up |
| Moon | 88.3° | −38.9° | Below the horizon: a crescent 20% lit, five days old, not yet risen |
| Mars | 160.0° | 83.1° | Nearly overhead, in daylight |
| Mercury | 103.3° | 5.0° | 7° under the Sun |
| Venus | 102.1° | −29.6° | An evening star, magnitude −4.3, below the horizon |

Jupiter and Saturn were below the horizon. The Moon and Venus that Schirra steered by over the
Indian Ocean on his third pass are the same two, seen from the other side of the day.

**The weather** (NASA TN D-4807, pp. 26 and 27), observed at the launch site at 7:17 a.m. EST:
cloud over five tenths of the sky (high cirrus at 30,000 ft, some low cumulus with its base at
2,200 ft, stratocumulus with its base at 4,500 ft on the eastern horizon), wind 2 knots from 150°,
visibility 10 miles, pressure 29.99 in Hg, 80 °F, dewpoint 75 °F, wet bulb 76.3 °F, humidity 85
per cent. The report's Figure 9 plots the wind to 60,000 ft. In the landing area, from USS
*Kearsarge* at the recovery: cloud over six tenths (cumulus from 3,000 ft, altocumulus at 14,000),
wind 11 knots from 110°, waves 3 ft from 090°, visibility 10 miles, the air 78 °F and the water
80 °F, 30.15 in Hg. Tropical storm Daisy lay 400 miles north-north-east of Puerto Rico, between
two of the Atlantic landing areas, one of which had been moved 213 nautical miles for it. No
`weather.ts` is written for this flight yet: it has no site model for a sky to stand over. These
are the figures one would be written from.

## What is not built

- **The launch site.** Complex 14 is [a site as numbers](../../sites/lc-14.ts): where it was, and
  which way the vehicle faced on it.
- **The pilot, standing**, and **a cabin to sit in**: the capsule's inside is modelled and cannot
  be boarded. The figure on the couch is the suit as modelled for Shepard with Schirra's name on
  it ([spacecraft.md](spacecraft.md), "The pilot").
- **The flight.** Nothing here can be flown. It wants the Atlas's weights, thrust and pitch
  programme, which have not been read; an orbit and an entry as data; drifting flight; a fuel
  budget worth keeping to. The numbers a simulator would start from are here: weights and moments
  of inertia for five phases of the flight are in NASA TN D-4807, Table I (p. 14).
- **The dipole antenna is not seen to go out.** Its two tapes are on their reels, as at liftoff.
- **The periscope is not seen to move.** Its door, objective and the link between them carry their
  motion, and `flight.ts` has when each went on this flight; with no clock in the gallery nothing
  plays them.

## Where sources disagree

- **The instant of liftoff.** 07:15:11 EST (NASA TN D-4807, p. 25); 07:15:11.84 (p. 3). The
  second sentence goes on to count all times "from 07:45:16.00 a.m. e.s.t. (range-zero time)",
  which is Aurora 7's range zero, left in from that flight's report. `flight.ts` carries 07:15:11.
- **How far from the carrier.** "Approximately 4 nautical miles" (TN D-4807, p. 2); "4.5 miles
  downrange" (*This New Ocean*, p. 483); "about 9,000 yards", which is 4.4 nautical miles, told to
  Schirra in the water (NASA SP-12, p. 119); sighted from the carrier at 5 nautical miles.
- **The landing point.** 32°06′ N 174°28′ W by integrating the trajectory, and 174°29′ W as the
  recovery ship reported the pickup (TN D-4807, Table XXIII and its note); the same report's
  Table XXVI gives the ship's report as 174°28′ W.
- **The fuel left after retrofire.** "68 [percent] auto and 78 [percent] manual", Schirra at
  08:52:50 (SP-12, p. 116); "68 percent in the automatic system and 84 percent in the manual"
  (SP-12, p. 18); "somewhere around 52 or 53 percent" automatic, and 80 per cent in each tank
  before the retrosequence, in the pilot's report (TN D-4807, p. 101).
- **Which shingles were instrumented.** "Two oppositely mounted bare shingles" (TN D-4807, p. 75;
  SP-12, p. 10: "two of the uncoated shingles"); "four thermocouples on the two instrumented
  shingles which had ablation material" (TN D-4807, p. 76).
- **The drogue's altitude.** 39,400 ft of pressure altitude (TN D-4807, p. 69); "at 40,000 feet"
  (the pilot's report, p. 103).
- **How fast and how high.** 25,730 ft/s at cutoff and 25,751 ft/s after the posigrade rockets,
  152.8 nautical miles (175.84 statute miles) at apogee (TN D-4807, Table XXIII); "176 miles" and
  "17,557 miles per hour" (*This New Ocean*, p. 473).
- **The staging backup timer.** "Changed from T + 136 to T + 132.3 seconds" from Aurora 7's
  Atlas (TN D-4807, p. 12); that vehicle's own report gives its backup as 132.2 seconds (NASA TN
  D-3814, p. 7).
- **What the low and high thrusters' switch did.** The report lists "a high-thruster arm-disarm
  switch ... added to the fly-by-wire (FBW) mode" (TN D-4807, p. 10) and, in its table of work
  done, "the low-thrust-only select switch" (p. 38). They are one switch: it kept the 24 lbf
  thrusters out of fly-by-wire until retrofire.

## Where the spacecraft is now

At the Kennedy Space Center Visitor Complex, in the Heroes and Legends hall (NASA
KSC-20161107-PH_GEB02_0276, 2016; in 1966 it was shown at the Manned Spacecraft Center, NASA
S66-53205). The shingle that carried the emblem has been photographed by itself, off the
capsule, its paint scorched (Commons: *Flown Superalloy Shingle with Sigma 7 logo*), and is what
the model's emblem is drawn from; who keeps it has not been read. The capsule as it is shown has
not been read against the model.
