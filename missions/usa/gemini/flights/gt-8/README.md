# Gemini-Titan 8 (Gemini VIII)

The first docking of two vehicles in orbit, and half an hour later the first emergency in orbit
that a crew had to fly their way out of: a thruster stuck open, a spin of nearly a turn a second,
and a landing in the Pacific ten hours and 41 minutes after liftoff, of a flight planned for three
days.

**In this repo: the vehicle, in the gallery, and its target beside it.** Spacecraft No. 8 on GLV-8
as they left the pad can be turned, taken apart as they staged, and cut open: the radar, the latch
receptacles and the index bar on its nose (the bar runs out at the end of the slider), the docking
light, the handrails along the adapter, the fuel cells and the doubled propellant tanks inside it,
and at its back the pack Scott was to have put on. The Agena it docked with stands on the floor
beside the launch vehicle, to scale, and can be stood alone (`#usa/gemini/gt-8/gallery/docking-target`),
taken apart and cut open. See "How the target is shown" below. At Complex 19 the vehicle stands on
the thrust mount on the morning of the flight, without the Agena, which went up from Complex 14: the
erector raised while the crew go up and laid down from T−55, Armstrong and Scott at the foot of the
ramp in their G4C suits, Scott's in its cover for going outside, each a figure to walk the site as,
under that morning's sky. Complex 14 and the flight itself are not built.

## The name

The flight is `usa/gemini/gt-8`, by its designation; its `callsign` is the mission's own style,
Gemini VIII. NASA allowed no names on its Geminis after Gemini-Titan 3's, and the ground called
this one by its numeral: "Gemini VIII, CSQ Cap Com" (*On the Shoulders of Titans*, pp. 233 and
314, as [history.ts](../../../../history.ts) has it).

## How the target is shown

The gallery shows one model of a flight, its vehicle ([gallery.md](../../../../../docs/agents/gallery.md)):
a second model would be built and never seen. So the Agena is part of the vehicle's own model, under
its own heading in the parts list, "Docking target". It stands on its engine's nozzle on the same
floor as the Titan's engines, 20 ft (6.1 m) from the Titan's axis on the side away from the
spacecraft's hatches. That is **an exhibit's arrangement**: the Agena left Complex 14 on an Atlas 101
minutes before this vehicle left Complex 19, and the two were never side by side on the ground.
The Agena is a vehicle of its own ([agena-target-vehicle.md](../../vehicles/agena-target-vehicle.md)):
this flight's model script chooses its unit, GATV-5003, and the model says which heading is its
target (`ModelDef.target`). So the gallery offers it alone, with its own slider and cutaway and its
twelve parts each with their words; it keeps its own floor while the launch vehicle beside it comes
apart; and where the spacecraft stands alone, here and in the Lineup, the Agena stands on the floor
beside it, the two at one scale. The Atlas, the adapter that stayed on the Atlas and the shroud over
the docking adapter are not built, nor is a docking flown.

## What happened

**The Agena.** The Atlas-Agena left Complex 14 at 15:00:03.127 G.m.t. The Agena's engine, whose
start had been changed after the one before it failed over the Atlantic in October, lit 377.5 s
after liftoff and put the vehicle into a near circle of 158.8 by 161.3 nautical miles; it weighed
7,116 lb there (mission report, 3.6, 4.1 and Table 4.2-II).

**The chase.** GLV-8 left Complex 19 one hour 40 minutes 59.262 seconds later, on an azimuth of
99.9°, and cut off at 337.54 s; the spacecraft was in an orbit of 86.3 by 146.7 nautical miles,
about 1,060 nautical miles behind the Agena. The crew kept the nose and scanner fairings on until
7 min 30 s. Four manoeuvres set up the coelliptic plan: 3.1 ft/s off the apogee at 1:34:36, 50.6
ft/s at 2:18:26 to raise the perigee, 26.6 ft/s square to the path at 2:45:53 for the plane, and
61.6 ft/s at 3:48:10 to make the orbit a near circle about 15 nautical miles under the Agena's (a
small correction of 2.3 ft/s at 3:03:42, the report says, should not have been made). The radar
first heard the Agena at 3:27:48 and held it from 181 nautical miles. The burn toward it began at
5:14:56, 29 nautical miles out; braking began at 5:43:09; by 5:55 the ship was about 150 ft from
the Agena, and the whole chase had cost 295 ft/s (mission report, 4.1, Table 4.3-III and 5.1.5).

**The docking.** For about 36 minutes the crew flew round the Agena at 150 to 50 ft and looked it
over. Then Armstrong brought the nose into the cone at about three quarters of a foot a second. At
6:33:16 the latches caught; 6.9 s after the ship engaged, the cone had drawn it in and the two were
rigid. "Flight, we are docked! It's ... really a smoothie" (*Titans*, p. 312).

**The roll.** At about 6:49 the Agena turned the pair 90° in yaw at the crew's command. At 7:00:26.7,
on the night side and out of touch with the ground, the valves of OAMS thruster No. 8 opened:
on for 4.9 s, off for 4.0, then on for the rest of the flight, "probably caused by an electrical
short to ground" (mission report, pp. 5-25 and 5-88). No. 8 yaws and rolls the ship left. The crew
took the trouble to be the Agena's, switched its control system off, fought the roll with the
ship's own thrusters, and at 7:15:12.3 undocked, backing out on thrusters 11 and 12 for 6.6 s.
Free of the Agena's mass the ship rolled faster: 296° a second within three minutes. "We have
serious problems here ... we're tumbling end over end up here," Scott told the tracking ship
*Coastal Sentry Quebec* (*Titans*, p. 314). Both men were dizzy, their sight blurred. Armstrong
shut the manoeuvring system off and opened both rings of the re-entry control system at 7:16:25.1;
its thrusters first fired at 7:19:03.8, the rates were under control by 7:25:30, and one ring was
shut again after 79.7 s of firing. Switching the manoeuvring thrusters back on one by one found
No. 8. It had fired for 900 s; between 7:00:26 and 7:25:30 about 200 lb of propellant had gone.

**Home.** About half the re-entry system's propellant was left (25 lb in one ring, 9 lb in the
other), and the rule was that a ship which has used that system lands at the next planned area.
Houston chose the western Pacific in the seventh revolution. The equipment section was cut away at
10:03:48, retrofire was at 10:04:47, the main parachute came out at 10:38:08, and the ship landed
at 10:41:26, less than 7 nautical miles from the point planned. The carrier *Boxer* was in the
Atlantic; the destroyer *Leonard F. Mason* reached the ship about three hours after it landed, and
the crew were aboard her at 06:28 G.m.t. on 17 March. Scott's walk outside, the three further
dockings and the firings of the Agena's engines with the ship docked were not made.

**The Agena afterwards.** Controllers flew it for days from the ground: ten manoeuvres with its two
propulsion systems, and 5,439 commands in all, 45 of them from the spacecraft (*Titans*, p. 320).

*On the Shoulders of Titans* (p. 309) and NASA SP-4002 have the liftoff at 10:41, an hour early; the
mission report and NASA's captions (S66-24465, "11:41 a.m. (EST)") are followed.

**What stood there.** The complex as the flights before left it: nothing read has any of it changed
([lc-19.md](../../../places/cape-canaveral/lc-19.md), "What stood when"). GLV-8 had stood in the erector
since 13 January. At liftoff the spacecraft's upper umbilical was not pulled by its drop weight, and
its lanyard to the tower pulled it free; "Pad damage was minimal and comparable to that of previous
launches", and GLV-9 stood on the same mount eight days later (5.2.10).

## What is not built

- **Of the launch site** ([lc-19.md](../../../places/cape-canaveral/lc-19.md), which lists it all): the people of
  the morning but the two who flew; the crew's van; the ventilator each man carried and his yellow
  overshoes. The white room's and the erector's times are the plan's, and how long each motion took
  is estimated.
- **Complex 14**, which the Agena left from that morning (issue #34): at the site the Agena is not
  drawn, and Complex 14 stands as the Cape's stand-in for it.
- **The Atlas** that carried the Agena, the adapter between them and the shroud.
- **The crew in their seats.** Armstrong and Scott stand on the approach to the ramp in the G4C
  ([g4c-suit.md](../../crew/g4c-suit.md)), Scott's let out over its cover for going outside; his overvisor
  is not built, and whether it was on his helmet at the pad is not settled (the mission report has it
  "added", the press kit "carried"). Scott's stature is not in any NASA source read: 6 ft is taken.
  The seats are empty.
- **The flight.** Flown from the pad to the landing as data (orbital.ts, [mission.md](mission.md)): the
  ascent, the rendezvous made on the Agena, the undocking, the early retrofire and the entry. The docking,
  the docked period, thruster No. 8 and the roll are not flown: the ship keeps station to the docking and
  coasts beside the Agena until it backs off. Fly still opens on the docking chapter, which docks.

## Not found

- **The Gemini VI mission report** of October 1965 (MSC-G-R-65-5 is the attempt's; the Agena's
  baseline description is its "ref. 6"), so the Agena here is from Lockheed's catalog, the launch
  systems' final report and this flight's report, which lists only differences.
- **Pages 3-29, 3-30, 4-7 and 4-8 of the mission report**: Figure 3.1-7 (the controls and displays)
  and Figure 4.1-1 are missing from the copy read.
- **A dimensioned drawing of the docking cone, of the index bar's installation, of the handholds
  and foot supports, or of the Extravehicular Support Package.**
- **This ship's own fuel cell reactant loads**: the manual's figures for a short mission are used.
- **Where the tape memory unit sat** in the adapter.
- **A close photograph of this vehicle's paint**, and what the Agena's far side looked like.
- **When the crew went aboard** on the morning. The count as announced had them in the ship at
  T−115 minutes and the hatches shut at T−100 (press kit, p. 10); the model's seats use that.

## Where the spacecraft is now

At the Armstrong Air and Space Museum in Wapakoneta, Ohio, by general knowledge; no record of it
has been read, and the flown ship has not been compared with the model.
