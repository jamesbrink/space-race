# Gemini-Titan 4 (Gemini IV)

Four days in orbit, the first American to leave his ship, and the first attempt to fly a
spacecraft up to another object in orbit, which failed and taught why.

**In this repo: the vehicle in the gallery, and its launch site.** Spacecraft No. 4 on GLV-4 as
they left the pad can be turned, taken apart as they staged, and cut open; the slider opens the
pilot's hatch and brings out what White went outside with. At Complex 19 it stands on the thrust
mount on the morning of the flight: the erector raised while the crew go up, stopping twice on
its way down and laid down at last after the hold, McDivitt and White on the approach to the ramp in
their G4C suits, each a figure to walk the site as, under that morning's clear sky. The flight
itself is not built: see "What is not built" below.

## The name

The flight is `usa/gemini/gt-4`, by its designation, as Gemini-Titan 3 is. After "Molly Brown"
NASA allowed no names: the flights from this one on are numbered, in Roman, and that is its
`callsign`. McDivitt and White had wanted to call the ship *American Eagle*; that is general
knowledge and has not been read in a source here.

## What happened

All of it is the mission report's (MSC-G-R-65-3) unless another source is named.

The count held for about an hour and a quarter at T-35 minutes: the erector, the tower that
stands a Titan up on Complex 19 and is lowered flat before the launch, stuck 12° from the vehicle
until a wrongly fitted connector in a junction box was found (*On the Shoulders of Titans*, p.
245). The report has the launch "approximately 1.24 hours later than planned"; *Titans* has a hold
of 1 hour 16 minutes. It was the first launch broadcast live to Europe, by the Early Bird
satellite, and the first flown from the control centre at Houston (the same page).

Both stages gave a little more thrust than nominal and cut off early: the first at 152.43 s, the
second at 333.75 s. The spacecraft cut itself free at 365.55 s, 31.8 s after the second stage's
cutoff, and five seconds on the aft thrusters left it in an orbit of 87.6 by 152.2 nautical miles.

**The chase.** The Titan's second stage was in almost the same orbit, with two flashing lights on
it for the purpose, and the plan was to fly beside it. McDivitt turned the ship round, found the
stage tumbling, and thrust toward it. In about 80 minutes he made 74 burns (the report lists each
one, by thruster and length, in its Table 4-IV), used 115 lb of propellant and about 102 ft/s, and
was never nearer than about 0.3 nautical mile, with the stage 5.1 miles off at the furthest. At
the end of the first revolution he was told to stop. *Titans* (pp. 246 and 248) gives the reason
it could not work: thrusting toward a target ahead raises the orbit, a higher orbit is a slower
one, and the target draws away; the way to catch it is to slow down, drop, and come up from below.
Nobody had yet reasoned that through, and there was no radar aboard to measure with.

**Outside.** Getting ready took longer than planned, and McDivitt put it off by one revolution.
The cabin was pumped down over Australia. The hatch would not unlatch at first: a spring that
returns the pawl of the latches' ratchet stuck (the report, 5.1.1.4). White stood up in the open
hatch, fixed a camera outside, and at about 4 hours 23 minutes pushed off on the hand-held
manoeuvring unit. He stopped himself 15 or 16 ft out, went back and forth twice, pitched and
yawed himself about, and after about four minutes had no gas left. For the rest he pulled on the
umbilical, which tended to swing him back over the adapter. McDivitt held the ship steady in
pulses so as to know when a thruster would fire. White was outside for about 22 minutes; told to
come in, he said, "It's the saddest moment of my life" (*Titans*, p. 249). He began to get in at
about 4 hours 46 minutes. Then the hatch would not latch: the handle swung free, the pack on his
chest was in the way, and the suit was pressed up harder than usual. McDivitt pulled on him to
give him purchase, the latches caught, and the cabin was pumped up by 5 hours 6 minutes. They did
not open it again to throw the equipment out, as had been planned.

**The rest.** The ship drifted for about two and a half days to save propellant, and the plan was
remade each day round what could be done drifting. At 75 hours 45 minutes 43 seconds the computer
would not switch off, and then would not work. So the entry was flown without it: the aft
thrusters burned for 2 minutes 41 seconds at 97:28:02 to bring the perigee down to 42.3 nautical
miles, the retrograde rockets fired at 97:40:01, a second early, and McDivitt set the ship rolling
steadily so that its lift cancelled. It pulled 7.7 g and landed at 97:56:12, 50 nautical miles
short of where the tracking had predicted.

The fault was the erector's own: after a moisture problem in the leg lock-unlock micro-switches was
put right following the wet mock simulated launch, "two wires were restored incorrectly" (the
report, 5.2.10). One umbilical, the first stage's fuel vent and topping line, did not let go and
was pulled free with the vehicle some 27 ft up. "The damage to the complex was minimal and not as
extensive as previous launches" (the same).

**What stood there.** The complex as Gemini 3 left it: nothing read has any of it changed between
23 March and 3 June 1965 ([lc-19.md](../../../places/cape-canaveral/lc-19.md), "What stood when").
NASA S65-29648 has the erector on its way down that morning, the vehicle standing in its usual
paint beside it; S65-29636 the liftoff and the lines hanging from the umbilical tower.

## The weather

**At the Cape, as recorded.** Cape Kennedy's weather station (the Skid Strip, about 3 km south of the
pad; NOAA's Integrated Surface Database, station 747940-12868) reported on the hour. The 15:00
report is the one nearest liftoff, 16 minutes before it.

| UTC (EST) | Wind | Air | Dew point | Sea-level pressure | Visibility | Sky |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 14:00 (9 a.m.) | 30° at 1.0 m/s (2 knots) | 25.5 °C | 13.9 °C | 1018.6 hPa | 16 km | clear |
| 15:00 (10 a.m.) | 70° at 2.6 m/s (5 knots) | 26.1 °C | 13.9 °C | 1018.6 hPa | 16 km | clear |
| 16:00 (11 a.m.) | 90° at 2.6 m/s (5 knots) | 26.1 °C | 14.4 °C | 1018.6 hPa | 16 km | clear |

All `documented`: fields of the record. The 16 km is the station's furthest mark, not how far one
could see. The total cover is coded 00 at every hour from 13:00 UTC (8 a.m.) to 19:00; at 12:00 it
had one okta at 1,200 m, gone by the next hour. Patrick Air Force Base, 30 km south (station
747950-12867), was clear from 12:00 on too, with 19 km visibility and the wind 60° at 2.1 m/s at
15:00.

**The mission report's** (MSC-G-R-65-3, 12.2): "Visibility was unlimited in all directions from the
Cape", and taken "at T-5 hours": clear skies, the wind from 060° at 7 knots, visibility 10 miles,
30.08 in. Hg, 81 °F with a dew point of 59 °F, 47 per cent humidity. Those figures fit the station's
late morning (1018.6 hPa is 30.08 in.; 81 °F is 27.2 °C, the station's at 17:00 UTC) better than its
hours before dawn, when it had 18 °C: which hour they are of is not clear, and the station's are
used.

`weather.ts` is written from these (no cloud, the wind 70° at 2.6 m/s, visibility taken as 50 km),
each with its confidence; the air aloft is ERA5's (`aloft.ts`).

## What is not built

- **Of the launch site** ([lc-19.md](../../../places/cape-canaveral/lc-19.md), which lists it all): the people of
  the morning but the two who flew; the crew's van; the ventilator each man carried and his yellow
  overshoes. When the erector stopped and how long each try took are estimated: only the order of
  them and the hold's length are recorded.
- **The crew in their seats.** McDivitt and White stand on the approach to the ramp in the G4C suit
  ([g4c-suit.md](../../crew/g4c-suit.md)), White's let out over its cover for going outside; his
  overvisor and thermal gloves are not built. Both men also sit in the cabin in their flight suits.
- **The flight.** No trajectory, no orbit as data, no simulator run: the instruments stand as
  built and the lamps are dark. The space walk is shown only as the hatch that opened and the
  equipment that went out through it.
- **The second stage as a target**, apart from its two lights.

## Not found

- **When the nose fairing went**, to the second: SEDR 300 says the command pilot pressed the switch
  after the second stage's cutoff on spacecraft 3 and 4.
- **When the hatches were shut** before the launch; the crew were aboard at T-100 minutes
  (*Titans*, p. 245). The press kit's count has no line for it.
- **When the erector stopped, was raised and stopped again**, to the minute: the history gives the
  order, the report the hold.
- **A photograph of the ship's sides** close enough to read its paint.
- **How far the hatch opened**, as a figure: 100° is by eye from the photographs.
- **Where the flashing lights were** round the second stage, and where the electrostatic charge
  experiment's sensor was in the adapter's skin.
- **The first stage's thrust as flown**: the report's section 5.2 has it and was not transcribed.

## Where the spacecraft is now

At the Smithsonian's National Air and Space Museum, by general knowledge; its record has not been
read, and the flown ship has not been compared with the model.
