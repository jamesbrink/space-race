# Gemini-Titan 7 (Gemini VII)

Fourteen days in orbit, in a suit made to be taken off; and on the eleventh of them, the target
that Gemini VI-A flew up to: the first time two crewed ships met.

**In this repo: the vehicle, in the gallery.** Spacecraft No. 7 on GLV-7 as they left the pad can
be turned, taken apart as they staged, and cut open: the transponder's antenna on its nose where
the last ship had a radar, the fuel cells and their tanks in the adapter, the third water tank,
the reserve fuel tank, the two lights at its foot, the four on its Titan. At Complex 19 it stands
on the thrust mount on the afternoon of the flight: the erector raised while the crew go up and
laid down at T−35, Borman and Lovell at the foot of the ramp in their G5C suits with the soft hoods
zipped on, each a figure to walk the site as, under that afternoon's sky. The flight itself is not
built: see "What is not built" below.

## The name

The flight is `usa/gemini/gt-7`, by its designation; its `callsign` is the mission's own style,
Gemini VII. NASA allowed no names after Gemini-Titan 3's, and this ship had none. *On the
Shoulders of Titans* calls this flight and Gemini VI-A's together the "Spirit of 76" mission, in
its foreword, and heads their chapter "Spirit of '76": the seventh and the sixth, flown as one.

## What happened

All of it is the mission report's (MSC-G-R-66-1) unless another source is named.

**How it came to be a target.** Gemini VII was planned as the long flight: fourteen days, to see
whether men would be fit for anything after as long as a trip to the Moon takes. Gemini VI was to
have flown first and met an Agena. The Agena was lost on its way up on 25 October 1965, and it
was decided to fly Gemini VII first and send Gemini VI-A up to it. Over a weekend
early in November spacecraft 7 was given what a target needs: a radar transponder with a voltage
booster and a spiral antenna in its nose, and two flashing lights on its adapter (*Titans*, pp.
279 and 280). Its flight plan gained two burns to make its orbit round at the height an Agena
would have had.

**The launch.** The count was nominal and the vehicle left 3.702 seconds after the planned second:
the sequencer that sends the ignition signal was to have been set three seconds ahead of the
range's clock and had been set level with it. The first stage flew slightly flatter than planned,
where every Gemini before had been lofted. Between 110 seconds and staging the vehicle shook
along its length a little, about 0.13 g for three seconds at 133 seconds; Borman noticed it and
Lovell did not. At staging the ship seemed to fly through a ring in an orange flame, which left a
film on the windows for the rest of the flight. The second stage cut off at 337.01 seconds, 7
ft/s slow and 175 ft high of the plan: "the nearest-to-nominal orbital-insertion conditions yet
achieved in the Gemini Program". The spacecraft cut itself free at 368.79 seconds, in an orbit of
87.2 by 177.1 nautical miles.

**The second stage.** GLV-7 carried four flashing lights round its second stage for this. Borman
thrust clear for two seconds, turned the ship round, thrust back, and flew beside the stage for
about a quarter of an hour at 60 to 150 ft. The stage was venting fuel hard from the foot of its
tank, which pushed it about and tumbled it at about two turns a minute, and staying with it cost
more propellant than planned. At 21 minutes 17 seconds, in the dark, the ship moved away. The
lights could be seen; nobody could judge distance by them, and the ship's own docking light did
not reach the stage.

**The oxygen.** The fuel cells' oxygen tank went up with its heater off and its pressure fell from
230 psia to about 140 in 22 minutes. This is what had nearly ended Gemini V, and spacecraft 7 had
been given a pipe from the crew's own oxygen tank to the fuel cells' because of it. At 25 minutes
the valve was opened for 17.5 seconds; about 0.35 lb of oxygen went across and the pressure rose
to 417 psia. After that the heater held it.

**Two weeks.** At 3 hours 47 minutes 59 seconds the perigee was raised to 119.6 nautical miles
with the aft thrusters, the ship powered down and lined up on the star Spica; at 69 hours 43
minutes the phase of the orbit was adjusted the same way, on Arcturus; and on the fifth day, with
the platform up, two burns at 119:11:55 and 119:55:01 left the ship in an orbit of 160.5 by 162.8
nautical miles, where it stayed. Both men slept at the same hours, on Houston's time. Eighteen
of the twenty experiments were done in whole or part; the two that needed a star photometer were
lost when it would not work. The ship used propellant faster than planned, and from the eighth
day it mostly drifted to keep enough for the rendezvous.

**The suits.** The G5C suit was made for this flight: 16 lb, a soft hood zipped on at the neck in
place of a helmet, and it could be taken off in the seat (*Titans*, p. 278; the press kit, p.
49). The crew had meant to take both off after two days. Headquarters ruled before the launch
that one man must be suited at all times (*Titans*, p. 281; SP-4002, p. 222). Lovell took his off
at 45 hours and was comfortable; Borman stayed in his and was hot. At 148 hours they changed
over. At about 191 hours, on 12 December, Headquarters let both be off together, and from then
the suits were worn only for the rendezvous (259 to 282 hours) and for the entry.

**The fuel cells.** A warning lamp for the pressure between oxygen and water in the second
section was on for most of the flight. From 127 hours that section's output fell and was brought
back four times, by purging it and by taking it off the line to rest; the report puts it down to
water gathering in the section. At 259 hours it fell again, and at 286 hours 57 minutes two of
its three stacks, 2A and 2C, were switched off for good. The other four stacks carried the load
to the end. The hydrogen tank, with its new insulation, never vented.

**Company.** Gemini VI-A's first try, on 12 December, ended on the pad. It left on 15 December at
13:37:26.47 G.m.t., which was 258:07:22.77 on this ship's clock; Borman and Lovell did not see the
liftoff but followed the climb. They put their suits on at about 259 hours and had one job: to
keep the ship's nose, and the transponder in it, toward the ship coming up. They first saw it as
a point of light about two miles off, and from half a mile could see its thrusters firing. The
rendezvous was complete at 263:58. The two flew together for about three and a half revolutions,
at one time a foot apart (*Titans*, p. 288), each crew telling the other of the cords trailing
from the back of its adapter. Borman and Lovell each flew station on the other ship for about
five minutes. At about 269:30 Gemini VI-A moved off, and next day it went home.

**The end.** At 283 hours 28 minutes the crew woke to find that thrusters 3 and 4, which yaw the
ship to the right, had all but failed; they yawed with a manoeuvring thruster instead. At about
290 hours the main fuel tank was empty and the reserve tank was opened. Retrofire came at
329:58:04, in the dark, as planned to the second. Borman flew the entry on one ring of the
re-entry thrusters, added the second in the blackout, and used all the propellant of both. The
ship landed 6.4 nautical miles short of the planned point and half a mile to the left, the
closest yet. The crew asked to be taken off by helicopter and were on the *Wasp* 32 minutes after
landing; the ship was aboard an hour after it came down.

## The afternoon at the pad

By the clocks at the Cape. One time of the afternoon at the pad is recorded: Lovell walking to the
erector's elevator "one hour and 40 minutes before launch" (NASA S65-61848), the count's T-100.
The rest is the press kit's plan (release 65-362, p. 4) put on the clock, which the day kept: the
mission report has no hold (5.2.10) and "the crew countdown went very well" (7.1.2.1). `derived`.

**What stood there.** The complex as Gemini 3 to 5 left it: nothing read has any of it changed
([lc-19.md](../../../places/cape-canaveral/lc-19.md), "What stood when"). What had changed was the work
on it: from GLV-7 on there was no wet mock simulated launch, so the erector stood from 29 October,
when it stood GLV-7 up, to the afternoon of the flight, the spacecraft wired to the Titan by a
jumper cable while it hung six feet over the second stage (NASA SP-4002, 29 October and 11 November
1965). The damage of the liftoff was "minimal", and GLV-6 stood on the same thrust mount by 11:00
the next morning (5.2.10; SP-4002, 4 December).

## The weather

altocumulus at 16 000 ft", the wind 340° at 10 knots, visibility 8 miles, 29.98 in. Hg, 70 °F with a
dew point of 61 °F, 73 per cent humidity. Its Table 12.2-I and Figure 12.2-1 have the launch area's

## What is not built

- **Of the launch site** ([lc-19.md](../../../places/cape-canaveral/lc-19.md), which lists it all): the people of
  the afternoon but the two who flew; the crew's van; the ventilator each man carried and his yellow
  overshoes. The afternoon's times are the plan's but one, and how long each motion took is
  estimated.
- **The crew in their seats.** Borman and Lovell stand at the foot of the ramp in the G5C, the soft
  hood zipped on ([g5c-suit.md](../../crew/g5c-suit.md)); the seats are empty.
- **The flight.** No trajectory, no orbit as data, no simulator run: the instruments stand as
  built and the lamps are dark. Gemini VI-A does not come.

## Not found

- **When the crew went aboard** and when the hatches were shut, as they went: the photograph has
  the crew at the elevator at T-100, and the hatches are put at the plan's T-75.
- **When the nose fairing and the scanners' cover went**: no time in the sequence of events.
- **When the equipment section was cut away and when the second, third and fourth retrograde
  rockets fired**: not recorded, the tape recorder having failed and no station being in reach
  (5.1.7.4).
- **A dimensioned drawing** of the transponder's antenna, the acquisition lights or water tank C:
  the report's figures (3.1-2, 3.1-4, 3.1-10) are sketches. The reserve fuel tank's size is in
  McDonnell's manual (SEDR 300 vol. 1, Section VIII, p. 8-271: 5.10 in by 30.7 in).
- **Which sides the two acquisition lights were on**, and how they were mounted: not in the
  report, and not made out in the three photographs of the ship that were read.
- **Where the docking light was** on the retrograde section, and where in the equipment section
  the spectrometer and the magnetometer's boom were.
- **A photograph of the launch vehicle** read for its paint and lettering. (The spacecraft's is
  read off the photographs Gemini VI-A's crew took: [spacecraft.md](spacecraft.md), "Paint".)
- **The period of the circular orbit** to the hundredth: the table's last digits are not legible
  (90.51 or 90.61 min).

## Where the spacecraft is now

At the Smithsonian's Steven F. Udvar-Hazy Center, by general knowledge; no record of it has been
read, and the flown ship has not been compared with the model.
