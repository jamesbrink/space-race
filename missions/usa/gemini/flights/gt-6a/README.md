# Gemini-Titan 6A (Gemini VI-A)

The first meeting of two crewed spacecraft in orbit, at the third count: an Agena target lost in
October, an engine shutdown on the pad on 12 December, and on the 15th a chase of four revolutions
that ended 120 ft from Gemini 7.

**In this repo: the vehicle, in the gallery.** Spacecraft No. 6 on GLV-6 as they left the pad can
be turned, taken apart as they staged, and cut open: the radar and the three latch receptacles
on its nose, the three batteries, the one water tank and the doubled propellant tanks in the
adapter. At Complex 19 it stands on the thrust mount on the morning of 15 December: the erector
raised while the crew go up and laid down at T−35, Schirra and Stafford at the foot of the ramp in
their G4C suits, each a figure to walk the site as, under that morning's sky. The flight itself is
not built: see "What is not built" below.

**The flight's own mission report has not been found.** *Gemini Program Mission Report, Gemini
VI-A*, MSC-G-R-66-2 (January 1966), was looked for on NTRS by title and through its API, on
archive.org, on ibiblio's Virtual AGC shelves and at the address nasa.gov's history pages would
give it; the one copy met is behind Scribd's wall (document 43455242) and was not read.
What is said here rests on two of its supplements, on the report of the October attempt, on the
Gemini VII report, on the conference papers its crew and planners gave two months later, and on
NASA's chronology and history, which cite the missing report page by page. Where a figure is a
secondary source's, it says so.

## The name

The flight is `usa/gemini/gt-6a`, by its designation; its `callsign` is the mission's own style,
Gemini VI-A. The sixth mission was to have been Gemini VI, a rendezvous and docking with an Agena.
When the Agena was lost and the mission was flown instead to Gemini 7, it kept its spacecraft, its
launch vehicle and its crew and took an A. The report on the October attempt, written in November,
already uses the letter: "Because of the early termination of this mission and to identify it from
other planned missions of the Gemini Program, this mission was designated Gemini VI-A"
(MSC-G-R-65-5, section 1.0). NASA allowed no names after Gemini-Titan 3's, and nothing was painted
on the ship (*On the Shoulders of Titans*, p. 233, as [history.ts](../../../../history.ts) has it).

## What happened

**25 October.** The Atlas-Agena left Complex 14 at 15:00:04.490 G.m.t. with Schirra and Stafford in
spacecraft 6 on Complex 19, due to follow it. Everything was normal until the
Agena lit its own main engine, 367.53 s after liftoff: the engine shut down at 368.44 s and all
telemetry and radar were lost at 375.71 s. Carnarvon never saw it, and the flight was cancelled
about 54 minutes after the Agena's liftoff; the Gemini count had been running normally (MSC-G-R-65-5,
section 1.0). The crew had been aboard since a quarter of an hour before the Atlas left (*Titans*,
p. 268).

**The change of plan.** The same day Walter Burke and John Yardley of McDonnell proposed flying
the ship to Gemini 7 instead (*Titans*, pp. 269 to 272). On 28 October the White House announced
that Gemini VI would be launched while Gemini VII was in orbit (SP-4002, 28 October 1965). GLV-6
was taken down and stored and the spacecraft "essentially placed in bonded storage" (*Gemini
Midprogram Conference*, p. 218). Gemini 7 left Complex 19 on 4 December; both stages of GLV-6 were
at the pad two hours later and standing within a day, the spacecraft came back on the 5th, its
computer was changed on the 7th and 8th, and the simulated flight was done by the 9th (SP-4002,
4 December 1965).

**12 December.** At 9:54 a.m. EST the first stage engine lit. About a second later an electrical
plug in the vehicle's tail fell out before the vehicle had risen. That started the vehicle's
programmer and the clock in the cabin as if it had lifted off; the ground equipment that runs the
count (the Master Operations Control Set) shut the engines down. The rule for a clock running over
dead engines was to eject. Neither man had felt the vehicle move, and Schirra, whose ring it was to
pull, did not pull it (*Titans*, p. 283; SP-4002, 12 December 1965). The erector was not back up
until 11:28 and the crew were out at 11:33. The engine's records then showed the thrust of one
chamber falling away before the shutdown was commanded: a plastic dust cap had been left in the
oxidiser inlet of that chamber's gas generator when it was cleaned at Martin's plant months before,
and would have stopped the engine about a second later in any case. It was found the next morning.
The cap was removed, the tail plugs were given a breakwire "to prevent premature dropout" (Gemini
VII mission report, 3.2.2.5), and a recycle that should have taken four days was done in three
(*Titans*, pp. 284 and 285).

**15 December: the chase.** Liftoff was at 13:37:26.471 G.m.t., on an azimuth of 81.4°, the second
stage steering in yaw to finish in Gemini 7's plane. It cut off at 338.73 s a little slow: the
orbit was 86.9 by 140.0 nautical miles where 146.2 was targeted, the ship some 1,200 miles behind
Gemini 7 and, at its first apogee, 23 nautical miles below it where 15 was wanted. The plan was the
"coelliptic" one: get into a circle 15 nautical miles under the target, wait until the target is at
a set angle above the horizon, then thrust along the line of sight and rise to it over 130° of
orbit. Five burns set it up, all on figures from the ground: 14 ft/s at the first perigee to lift
the apogee; 61 ft/s (53 planned) at the second apogee to lift the perigee and set the rate of
catching up; 34 ft/s square to the path for the plane; 0.8 ft/s at the next perigee; and 43 ft/s at
the third apogee, at 3 h 47 min, to make the circle (*Midprogram Conference*, pp. 279 to 281). The
radar had been switched on at three hours and locked solidly on Gemini 7's transponder at 232
nautical miles (the same, p. 34).

From there the crew flew it with the radar, the computer and their own charts. At 5 h 4 min Schirra
took Gemini 7, in sunlight a hundred kilometres ahead, for Sirius (*Titans*, p. 287). In the dark
that followed they could not see it at all until 25.7 nautical miles, when its docking light showed
faintly; the flashing lights put on Gemini 7 for the purpose did not show until 14.5
(*Midprogram Conference*, p. 291). The burn that began the last climb came 19,139 s after liftoff
(5 h 18 min 59 s), two midcourse corrections followed at about 5 h 32 min and 5 h 44 min, and at
5 h 50 min, 3,000 ft out and closing at about 40 ft/s, Schirra began to brake. By 1,500 ft the
closing speed was 7 ft/s. About six minutes after the braking began the ship was stopped 120 ft
directly below Gemini 7: 2:33 p.m. EST (*Titans*, p. 287).

**Beside Gemini 7.** For about five and a half hours, more than three revolutions, the ships flew
together at 1 ft to 300 ft. Spacecraft 6 flew round Gemini 7, faced it nose to nose through a night
pass at 6 to 18 m, and at one point held its place for twenty minutes with nobody touching the
stick; a change of speed of a tenth of a foot a second could be made at will (*Titans*, p. 288).
Each crew told the other that cords were streaming from the back of its adapter: the holders of the
charge that cuts the ship from the Titan (Gemini VII mission report, 5.1.9, which identifies what
was seen on that ship). Each photographed the other; in one picture (NASA S65-64040, taken from Gemini 7 at
about 38 ft) a blue card in spacecraft 6's right-hand window reads BEAT ARMY. Then Schirra backed
away for the night.

**Home.** With everything done, the flight came home after one day. Before retrofire Stafford
reported "an object, looks like a satellite going from north to south, probably in polar orbit",
and the two played "Jingle Bells": Schirra on a four-hole harmonica he had been given on 8
December, Stafford on small bells (*Titans*, p. 289). Retrofire was at 25:15:58, at night. Schirra
held the ship banked 55° left until the computer's guidance took over at about 85 km and then flew
its needles; the ship landed 7 nautical miles from the planned point, the first controlled
re-entry to succeed, in view of television from the *Wasp*, and was hoisted aboard with the crew
in it (*Titans*, pp. 289 and 291; SP-4002, 15 December 1965). The one failure of note was the tape
recorder for delayed telemetry, at 20 h 55 min.

## The morning at the pad

By the clocks at the Cape. With the mission report not found, no time of the morning at the pad is
in what was read but the liftoff's. The press kit's count for Gemini 6 (release 65-362, p. 29) had
a "25-minute hold" built in at T-3, so that the Titan could be let go on the second Gemini 7 came
over; the launch systems final report has no hold on the day (Table I-1), which is taken to mean the
built-in one was taken whole and nothing more. Put on the clock so, the plan has the crew going up
in the dark, which NASA S65-59974 (the two leaving the suiting trailer) bears out. Every time below
is `estimated`; the pad's slider plays them ([pad.ts](pad.ts)).

**Three days before.** On Sunday 12 December the crew went aboard for the second time, the count ran
clean, and at 9:54 the engines lit: an electrical plug in the Titan's tail dropped out too soon, the
airborne programmer started, and the malfunction detection system stopped the engines 1.2 s later,
the vehicle still bolted to the thrust mount. Schirra did not pull the ejection ring. The erector was
not raised round them again until 11:28, emergency procedures first, and they were out at 11:33
(*On the Shoulders of Titans*, pp. 283 and 284; NASA SP-4002, 12 December). The next morning a dust
cap was found left in a gas generator's oxidizer inlet, which would have shut the engine down
anyway; the plugs were given safety wire. The launch was made in three days instead of four.

**What stood there.** The complex as Gemini 7 left it eleven days before: GLV-6 stood on the same
thrust mount by 11:00 the morning after that liftoff, the damage "minimal", and the workmen "wasted
little time on normal painting or cleaning" (*Titans*, p. 282; the Gemini VII mission report,
5.2.10). Nothing read has any structure changed ([lc-19.md](../../../places/cape-canaveral/lc-19.md),
"What stood when"); the scorch of Gemini 7 is not drawn.

## What is not built

- **Of the launch site** ([lc-19.md](../../../places/cape-canaveral/lc-19.md), which lists it all): the people of
  the morning but the two who flew; the crew's van; the ventilator each man carried and his yellow
  overshoes. Every time of the morning is estimated, from the plan. The shutdown of 12 December is
  told, not played.
- **The crew in their seats.** Schirra and Stafford stand at the foot of the ramp in the G4C suit
  (*Midprogram Conference*, p. 64; [g4c-suit.md](../../crew/g4c-suit.md)); the seats are empty. The
  flight's hexagonal emblem (NASA S65-55983), on the suits by NASA S65-61916, is not built.
- **The flight.** No trajectory, no orbit as data, no simulator run: the instruments stand as
  built and the lamps are dark. Nothing here chases Gemini 7.
- **The other two counts.** The model is the vehicle of 15 December. The Atlas-Agena of 25 October
  and Complex 14 are not built.

## Not found

- **The mission report itself** (MSC-G-R-66-2), and with it: the launch area's weather and when
  each thing was done at the pad on 12 and 15 December; what spacecraft 6 changed from
  spacecraft 5, as its section 3.1 would list it; GLV-6's changes from GLV-5; the weights of the
  vehicle at liftoff and of the ship at retrofire and landing; the ascent's sequence of events
  (engine start, staging, the fairings, separation) from the vehicle's own telemetry; what was
  loaded into the OAMS and what was used; the time of each manoeuvre to the second.
- **Whether the docking fittings flew.** The ship was built with an index bar and three latch
  receptacles for its Agena. Nothing read says they were taken off for a flight with nothing to
  dock to, and it sat in bonded storage between the two; they are taken to have flown.
- **Where this ship's OAMS tanks stood.** That it had two of fuel, two of oxidiser and two of
  helium, each the size of the one the ships before had, is in SEDR 300's second volume (Section
  VIII, Figures 8-94 and 8-96); the drawing of where is a sketch in perspective.
- **Where the docking light was**, and the radar's own dimensions.
- **Why the card said what it said**, and who put it in the window: the photograph is the source.
- **When the crew went aboard on the 15th**, and a photograph of the vehicle close enough to read
  its paint.

## Where the spacecraft is now

At the Stafford Air & Space Museum in Weatherford, Oklahoma, by general knowledge; no record of it
has been read, and the flown ship has not been compared with the model.
