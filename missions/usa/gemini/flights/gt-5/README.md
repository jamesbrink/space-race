# Gemini-Titan 5 (Gemini V)

Eight days in orbit on the first fuel cells to power a crew, which nearly ended the flight on its
first day; the first rendezvous radar, and a target for it that was thrown out and never caught.

**In this repo: the vehicle in the gallery, and its launch site.** Spacecraft No. 5 on GLV-5 as
they left the pad can be turned, taken apart as they staged, and cut open: the radar on its nose,
the fuel cells and their tanks in the adapter, the pod hung in its middle. At Complex 19 it stands
on the thrust mount on the morning of the flight: the erector raised while the crew go up and laid
down at T−35, Cooper and Conrad at the foot of the ramp in their G4C suits, each a figure to walk
the site as, under that morning's sky. The flight itself is not built: see "What is not built" below.

## The name

The flight is `usa/gemini/gt-5`, by its designation; its `callsign` is the mission's own style,
Gemini V. NASA allowed no names after Gemini-Titan 3's. Cooper asked instead for an emblem and
got one, the first an American crew wore: a covered wagon, after a model Conrad's father-in-law
had whittled. The motto meant to go with it, "Eight days or bust", the Administrator turned down,
in case the flight did not last eight days. The crew called the ship the Covered Wagon; it was
painted nowhere (*On the Shoulders of Titans*, pp. 256 and 258).

## What happened

All of it is the mission report's (MSC-G-R-65-4) unless another source is named.

The launch had been set for 19 August and was given up that morning with the crew aboard: a
thunderstorm came over the pad, lightning struck near the power supply, and the spacecraft's
computer faltered. The propellants were drained and the count recycled 48 hours (*Titans*, p.
256). On the 21st the vehicle left 0.482 s before the planned instant.

In the first stage's flight the crew were shaken along the vehicle's length at up to 0.38 g, over
the limit of a quarter of a g for about 13 seconds: the oxidiser standpipes that suppress the
oscillation had been charged with a tenth of the gas they needed. It was the only time a Gemini
crew met it (*Titans*, p. 256, footnote). The fairing over the radar and the scanners' cover went
at 207 s. Both stages cut off early; the spacecraft separated at 356.91 s into an orbit of 87.4 by
188.9 nautical miles, and at 56 minutes raised its perigee to 92.4.

**The oxygen.** The fuel cells take hydrogen and oxygen from two tanks in the adapter, each kept
supercritical, and a heater in each tank holds the pressure up as it is emptied. At 25 minutes 51
seconds the oxygen tank's heater circuit shorted, at 853 psi; the breaker tripped, and when it was
reset the heater drew no current. The pressure fell. It was still well above what the regulator
needed when the pod was due, so the pod was thrown out on time, at 2 hours 7 minutes 15 seconds,
at 4.6 ft/s with the ship yawed square to its path, and the radar locked on and followed it out to
7,721 ft. Ten minutes later, over Carnarvon, the crew reported the pressure falling fast: 330 psi
there, 116 over Hawaii eighteen minutes after. The ship was powered down to about 13 amperes and
the pod left. Nobody knew how low a fuel cell would go on working; a landing at the sixth
revolution was made ready, about two hours off. The pressure steadied near 70 psi, where the
oxygen in the tank was partly liquid; a cell run at low pressure at St. Louis kept working
(*Titans*, p. 257); and on the seventh revolution the ship was powered up again, a little at a
time. The pressure crept back to 260 psi by the end of the flight.

**The rest.** On the second day the radar locked on to a transponder on the ground at Cape Kennedy
and read it well. On the third day, in place of the pod, the crew flew four burns toward a point
where an Agena might have been: lower the apogee, adjust the phase, change the plane, make the
orbits concentric, the last two worked out aboard. The fuel cells' hydrogen had to be vented, and
the venting turned the drifting ship. On the fifth day the manoeuvring system grew sluggish and
thruster 7 stopped, then thruster 8; from there on the ship mostly drifted, with the power down,
and the crew were cold. Retrofire came at 190:27:43, in darkness, one revolution earlier than
planned because of weather in the landing area. The entry was steered wrongly through no fault
aboard: the coordinates sent up for it had left out a term, and the ship landed 89 nautical miles
short. Cooper saw that the needle disagreed with what he knew and flew the rest by hand.

## The morning at the pad

By the clocks at the Cape. No time of the morning at the pad is in what was read: the mission report
has the crew in "at the proper time" (7.1.1.1) and a count "completely nominal" with no hold
(6.1.1.4), so each time below is the press kit's plan (release 65-262: the crew's morning on p. 13,
the count on pp. 22 and 23) put on the clock, which the day kept: `derived`. The pad's slider
plays it ([pad.ts](pad.ts)).

| EST | Event | Confidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| about 7:11 | T-1 h 49 min: the crew leave the ready room at Complex 16 | derived |
| about 7:16 | T-1 h 44 min: at the 100 ft level of the erector | derived |
| about 7:20 | T-100: in the spacecraft | derived |
| about 8:20 | T-40: the white room empty, the erector cleared to lower | derived |
| about 8:25 | T-35: the erector starts down (NASA S65-49568 has it part way) | derived |
| 8:59:59.518 | liftoff, 0.482 s early | documented |

The crew felt "a vibration ... introduced into the vehicle" during the erector's lowering
preparations, which was never explained (5.2.10.3). It was the second try. On 19 August, after a hold
at T-10 at 12:08 EST, the erector was raised again and the count cancelled at 12:41 with a
thunderstorm over the pad (5.2.10.1; *On the Shoulders of Titans*, p. 256).

**What stood there.** The complex as Gemini 3 and 4 left it: nothing read has any of it changed
([lc-19.md](../../../places/cape-canaveral/lc-19.md), "What stood when"). "Pad damage was minimal"
(5.2.10.3). NASA S65-28737 and S65-28739 have the two men on the crew's ramp at pad 19 with the
transfer van parked on the deck behind them (not built).

The figures are `documented`; the sky's classes are read as Gemini-Titan 3's README says. Patrick Air
Force Base, 30 km south (747950-12867), had the wind 70° at 3.6 m/s at 14:00, scattered.

**The mission report's** (MSC-G-R-65-4, 12.2, p. 12-8), "taken at 09:00 e.s.t.": "2/10 covered,
scattered at 8000 feet", the wind calm, visibility 10 miles, 30.09 in. Hg, 87 °F with a dew point of
74 °F, 66 per cent humidity: the station's 09:00 observation, to the degree. Its Table 12.2-I has
the air at 14:00 G.m.t. (86 °F at the ground, 62 °F at 5,000 ft, 46 °F at 10,000 ft), and Figure
12.2-1 the winds aloft, not transcribed.

`weather.ts` is written from these (two tenths of altocumulus at 2,440 m, a sixth or less of thin
cirrus, the air all but still: calm is under a knot, and 0.3 m/s is taken from the east), each with its confidence; the air aloft is ERA5's (`aloft.ts`). No low cloud is
drawn: the report has none at liftoff, and the station's cumulus at 660 m is of the hour after.

## What is not built

- **Of the launch site** ([lc-19.md](../../../places/cape-canaveral/lc-19.md), which lists it all): the people of
  the morning but the two who flew; the crew's van; the ventilator each man carried and his yellow
  overshoes. The morning's times are the plan's, and how long each motion took is estimated.
- **The crew in their seats.** Cooper and Conrad stand at the foot of the ramp in the G4C suit,
  without the outer cover Gemini IV's pilot had ([g4c-suit.md](../../crew/g4c-suit.md)); the seats are
  empty. The red-lettered label under Cooper's name tag (S65-28740) and the flight's emblem, whose
  place on the suits nothing read gives, are not built.
- **The flight.** No trajectory, no orbit as data, no simulator run: the instruments stand as
  built and the lamps are dark. The pod does not fly; it sits where it was carried.
- **The flight azimuth** is not given as a figure here: it is not in the parts of the report that
  were read. The inclination is Gemini IV's to six hundredths of a degree, and that flight's
  azimuth was 72°.

## Not found

- **Any time of the morning at the pad**, as it went: when the crew reached pad 19, went aboard,
  when the hatches were shut, the erector started down and lay down. Not in the mission report, *On
  the Shoulders of Titans* or the *Chronology*; *Spaceport News* of 26 August 1965 was not reached.
- **The pod's size and weight**, and a drawing of where it sat: its form is scaled from one sketch.
- **The fuel cell sections' and the two tanks' sizes** as figures: SEDR 300's second volume gives
  the tanks' loads and not their dimensions.
- **Whether this ship's OAMS tanks were larger** than the early ships': *Titans* (p. 248) says
  spacecraft 4's were half the size of later ones, without saying when they grew.
- **Where the docking light was** on the retrograde section, and where the radiometry experiments'
  sensors were in the adapter.
- **What was loaded into the OAMS**, which is in a table of the report that was not transcribed.
- **When the crew went aboard**, and a photograph of the ship's sides close enough to read its paint.

## Where the spacecraft is now

At Space Center Houston, by general knowledge; no record of it has been read, and the flown ship
has not been compared with the model.
