# Gemini-Titan 3, "Molly Brown"

The first Gemini with a crew: three times round the Earth, and the first time the people aboard a
spacecraft changed the orbit they were in.

**In this repo: the vehicle, in the gallery.** Spacecraft No. 3 on GLV-3 as they left the pad can
be turned, taken apart as they staged, and cut open. Its launch site, its crew and the flight
itself are not built: see "What is not built" below.

## The flight's id, and the name

The flight is `usa/gemini/gt-3`. Mercury's flights here carry their spacecraft's names
(`ma-6-friendship-7`) because those names were official: they were painted on the capsules and are
what the reports call the flights. This one was not. Grissom chose "Molly Brown", after the
musical *The Unsinkable Molly Brown*, his Mercury capsule having sunk. NASA thought it lacked
dignity, gave way when his second choice turned out to be "Titanic", and let it stand "only
quasi-officially" (*On the Shoulders of Titans*, p. 233). It was used on the radio ("You're on
your way, Molly Brown") and painted nowhere: no photograph read shows a name on the ship. After it
NASA named no Gemini, and the flights from Gemini IV on are numbered. So the id is the
designation, as the Vostoks' are, and the later flights can follow it (`gemini-4`, or as they are
decided). The name is the flight's `callsign`, which is what the page calls it.

## What happened

Two unmanned Geminis had flown. This one was to show that the ship could carry two men, that they
could steer it in orbit, and that it could be flown down to a chosen place on the sea.

The Titan was filled the evening before. The count began at two in the morning under cloud. Grissom
and Young were woken a little before five, ate steak and eggs, suited up at pad 16, and were in the
spacecraft with the hatches shut at 7:30, twenty minutes early. Thirty-five minutes before the
launch the first stage's oxidiser line was found to be leaking; a wrench on a badly seated nut
stopped it, and the count was held for 24 minutes to be sure. The cloud broke while they waited.
The engines started, and four seconds later, at 9:24:00, the vehicle was let go.

Neither man felt it leave. The first stage burned for two and a half minutes, to six times their
weight; then, with the stages still joined, the second stage's engine lit, and orange light
flooded the windows. Five and a half minutes up the engine stopped, a charge cut the ship from the
Titan, and Grissom pushed on with the aft thrusters, a little too long. Only then, in orbit, did
the nose fairing and the scanner cover go: the press kit had them going three minutes up, but on
this ship the switch was pressed after the engine had stopped.

Twenty-four minutes into the flight the oxygen pressure gauge dropped, and then several others.
Young took 45 seconds to decide it was the instruments and not the oxygen, and switched their power
supply over. Grissom turned the handle on the sea urchin experiment and it came off in his hand.
Young worked the blood experiment, and tried the freeze-dried food, and then produced a corned
beef sandwich that Schirra had bought at Wolfie's in Cocoa Beach. Grissom ate a
few bites and put it away because of the crumbs. It was the only part of the flight Congress asked
about.

An hour and 33 minutes up Grissom fired the forward thrusters for 74 seconds and took 15 metres a
second off the ship's speed: a nearly round orbit. Three quarters of an hour later he shifted the
orbit's plane by a fiftieth of a degree. The ship could push only forward and back, its sideways
thrusters being dummies, so the plan had it turned square to its path, and the forward thrusters
and then the aft ones fired (press kit, p. 7); and so he flew it. On the third
orbit a burn of a minute and fifty seconds brought the low point down to 45 nautical miles, 83 km:
had the retrograde rockets failed after that, the air would have brought the ship in regardless.

They did not fail. The adapter's equipment section was cut away, the four rockets fired one after
another at 4 h 33 min 23 s, the retrograde section went, and Molly Brown came in. Gemini's heat
shield is set off-centre so that the ship flies at an angle and makes lift, and by rolling it the
crew were to steer for the *Intrepid*. But the ship made far less lift than the wind tunnels had
said, and nothing Grissom did could stretch the path. It came down 60 nautical miles short.

Under the parachute the ship is swung from hanging by its nose to hanging level. When Grissom
threw the switch it dropped so hard that both men were thrown into their windows: his faceplate
broke and Young's was scratched. In the water the parachute dragged the nose under until he let
it go. The carrier was 110 km away, so they asked for a helicopter; and Grissom, who had lost
Liberty Bell 7 through an open hatch, kept this one's shut until swimmers had a collar round it.
They sat for half an hour in a closed, hot ship pitching on the swell. "That was no boat," Young
said.

## Timeline

The flight as flown, in time from liftoff. The mission report, which has every event to the tenth
of a second, has not been found; these are from what has.

Sources, short. **Transcript**: the composite air-to-ground and onboard voice transcript (*GT-3
Communications Transcript*, NASA MSC, April 1965), whose first column is elapsed time from launch
to the second. A time "as called" is when the words were said, a moment after the event.
**IGS**: the guidance reconstruction, supplemental report 4 to the mission report. **SP-121**: the
*Gemini Midprogram Conference*. **SP-138**: the *Gemini Summary Conference*. **SP-4002**: the
*Chronology*, Appendix 1, Table A. **SP-4203**: *On the Shoulders of Titans*. **LSFR**: the
*Gemini Program Launch Systems Final Report*. **SEDR 300**: McDonnell's familiarisation manual.

The same events, in seconds, are `timeline` in [flight.ts](flight.ts).

**Staging is derived, and assumes one thing**: that the press kit's 2 min 36 s (156 s) is the
nominal time the launch systems report's table of dispersions measures from. The table has this
vehicle's first stage cutting off 1.70 s early and its second 4.68 s early. For the second stage
the assumption can be checked and holds: the press kit's 5 min 38 s is 338 s, SP-121's planned
insertion less its 20 s is 338.4 s, and 338.4 less 4.68 is 333.7, the 334 s of the guidance
reconstruction. Grissom's call at 2:35 is a second after 154.3.

**The ascent was not the press kit's.** Its plan (release 65-81, p. 11) was staging at 2:36,
fairing jettison at 3:15, second stage cutoff at 5:38 and separation at 5:58. The fairings did not
go at 3:15 and were not meant to: "The Command Pilot presses the switch after SECO on S/C 3 and 4,
and after staging on S/C 7" (SEDR 300, p. 4-53). The engines were to start 4 s before liftoff (the
same page of the press kit); when they did is not in anything read.

The morning, by the clocks at the Cape (SP-4203, pp. 232 and 233): oxidiser loading from 6:22 p.m.
on the 22nd, all tanks full five hours later; the count from 2 a.m.; the crew woken shortly before
5, at pad 16 about 6, suited by about 6:45, in the van shortly after 7, in the spacecraft with the
hatches sealed at 7:30; the hold of 24 minutes at T-35; liftoff at 9:24.

## The weather

**At the Cape, as recorded.** Cape Kennedy's weather station (the Skid Strip, call letters XMR,
about 3 km south of the pad; NOAA's Integrated Surface Database, station 747940-12868) reported on
the hour. The 14:00 report is the one nearest liftoff, 24 minutes before it.

| UTC (EST) | Wind | Air | Dew point | Sea-level pressure | Visibility | Sky |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 13:00 (8 a.m.) | 130° at 4.6 m/s (9 knots) | 21.6 °C | 18.3 °C | 1024.0 hPa | 16 km | scattered, no ceiling |
| 14:00 (9 a.m.) | 140° at 5.7 m/s (11 knots) | 22.8 °C | 18.9 °C | 1024.7 hPa | 16 km | scattered, no ceiling |
| 15:00 (10 a.m.) | 150° at 7.2 m/s (14 knots) | 23.3 °C | 18.3 °C | 1025.1 hPa | 16 km | scattered, no ceiling; 2/8 of altocumulus at 3,300 m |

The wind, temperatures, pressure, visibility and ceiling are `documented`: they are fields of the
record. The 16 km is the station's furthest mark (10 statute miles), not how far one could see.
The sky is `derived`, our decoding of the record's cloud groups (docs/sources.md gives them raw):

- **The cover at 14:00 is "scattered" and no more exact than that.** The hourly code is 02, which
  the database's format calls two oktas. But at both stations it takes only the values 02, 07 and
  08 all day, the airways classes scattered, broken and overcast, and at 12:00 it stands at 02 over
  layers that add up to five oktas. So it is read as a class: between a tenth and a half of the sky.
- **The layers are reported every third hour.** At 12:00 UTC (7 a.m. EST): 1/8 of cumulus at 480 m,
  1/8 of altocumulus at 2,700 m, and 3/8 of cirrostratus above, whose height is coded at the
  record's limit and was not measured. At 15:00: 2/8 of altocumulus at 3,300 m and nothing else.
  Liftoff lies between the two. From 17:00 UTC the sky was broken with a ceiling at 3,000 m.

**Earlier that morning.** The same station had 6/8 with a ceiling at 900 m at 06:00 UTC (1 a.m.
EST), an overcast with its ceiling at 1,080 m at 07:00, scattered cloud and no ceiling at 08:00,
and 3/8 at 1,050 m at 09:00. Patrick Air Force Base, 30 km south of the pad (station
747950-12867), had broken cloud with a ceiling at 900 m at 08:00 and 09:00 UTC (5/8 at 09:00),
scattered at 10:00, broken at 2,400 m at 11:00, and scattered cloud in haze from 12:00; at 14:00
its wind was 150° at 6.2 m/s and its air 21.8 °C. All `derived` in the same way.

**Against the history.** *On the Shoulders of Titans* (pp. 232 and 233) has the count beginning at
2 a.m. "under overcast skies", which is the Cape's 07:00 UTC report to the hour; the overcast "not
having lifted as expected" when the crew were aboard at 7:30; the clouds beginning to scatter by
the end of the hold; and the sky "almost clear" at 9:24, which is the scattered cloud of 14:00 and
the two oktas of 15:00. What the stations do not show is a ceiling of any kind while the crew were
aboard: after Patrick's broken cloud at 11:00 UTC (6 a.m.) neither reported one, on the hour,
until the afternoon. The three oktas of high cloud at 7 a.m. may be what was meant; nothing read
says.

**Against the rules.** The press kit's guidelines for the launch area were surface winds of 18
knots with gusts to 25, a ceiling of at least 5,000 ft and visibility of 6 miles (p. 9). The
morning was inside all three.

**In the landing area**, no observation has been read. What the crew were told is in the voice
transcript: at 3 h 08 min the ground passed up the weather for area 4-1, "broken cloud conditions.
20 miles visibility. Wind is 20 knots, at 5 foot seas" (p. 77), and under the parachute, at an
indicated 2,000 ft, Grissom said "We're coming through the lower layer" (p. 104). `documented` as
said; the sea and the sky at the landing itself are in the mission report.

No `weather.ts` is written for this flight yet: it has no site model for a sky to stand over.
These are the figures one would be written from.

## What is not built

- **The launch site.** Complex 19 is [a site as numbers](../../sites/lc-19.ts): where it was, and
  which way the vehicle is taken to have faced on it, which is not settled.
- **The crew.** The two ejection seats are empty. A Gemini G3C suit has not been modelled, and the
  Mercury suit is a different suit: no figures have been put in it in their place.
- **A cabin to sit in.** The inside is laid out for the cutaway and cannot be boarded.
- **The flight.** Nothing here can be flown. It wants the Titan's weights and thrust, the pitch
  programme, an orbit and three burns as data, and a lifting entry whose lift is what was flown and
  not what was predicted.
- **The nose fairing is not seen to go**, nor the adapter to be cut away in its two parts: the
  gallery takes the vehicle apart in that order and no clock plays it.

## Where sources disagree

- **The first orbit: 87.0 by 121.0 nautical miles (161 by 224 km), `documented`.** Two tables
  agree: SP-121, Table 4-III, p. 33 (planned 87.1 by 130.1; the apogee came out 9.1 low), and
  SP-4002, Appendix 1, Table A. SP-4203's own Appendix B (p. 524) has the same apogee, "224 km
  (121 n.m.)", as the flight's highest; the "158.5 km (85.6 n.m.)" beside it is the flight's
  lowest perigee, as in SP-4002's table, and is the perigee the ground called up after the first
  burn ("85.6, 92.6"), not the perigee at insertion. **The outlier is SP-4203's narrative**, "122
  by 175 kilometers, very close to the intended 122 by 182" (p. 235), which agrees with neither
  its appendix nor the plan in any unit tried; it is not followed. Two other figures are not the orbit as tracked: the ground's first
  call to the crew, "87 by 125" at 7 min 23 s, and the spacecraft's own guidance, 78 by 128
  (SP-121, the same table). The press kit's plan was 100 by 150 statute miles (161 by 241 km).
- **How far from the planned point it landed: 60 nautical miles (111 km), `documented`.** SP-138,
  Table 13-I, p. 163, with a navigation error of 0.8; SP-4002, Table A and p. 189; SP-4203,
  Appendix B, p. 524 ("111.1 km (60 n.m.)"). **The outlier is again SP-4203's narrative**, "about
  84 kilometers short" (p. 236). Eighty-four kilometres is 45 nautical miles, and 45 nautical
  miles is, on the same page of SP-138, the vacuum perigee the third burn left: a height, not a
  distance along the sea. It is taken as a mix-up of the two and not followed. Why it fell short
  is on that page too: the ship made about 35 per cent less lift for its drag than predicted,
  which cost about 160 nautical miles of reach; flown on the computer's commands throughout it
  would have missed by about 3.
- **The perigee after the third burn: 45 nautical miles (83 km) in vacuum, `documented`** (SP-138,
  p. 163; the press kit planned "52 miles", statute, which is the same height: p. 10). SP-4203 has
  "72 kilometers" (p. 235), which is 45 statute miles: the same number in the wrong mile, it
  seems. SP-138 is followed.
- **The third burn's length.** "A two and a half minute burn" (SP-4203, p. 235). The voice
  transcript has it from 4:21:23 to 4:23:14, 111 s, timed by the crew at "a minute and 48 seconds";
  the ground had asked for 96 ft/s in 1 min 49 s. The transcript is followed. Two minutes 39
  seconds, for 139 ft/s, was the length of a pre-retrofire burn in a load read up to the crew at
  52 minutes, before the first burn had changed the orbit; that load was not the one flown.
- **The first burn.** 74 s by the crew's watches, 1:33:00 to 1:34:14, and about 49 ft/s by their
  indicator (transcript); "a carefully timed 75 seconds" and 15 m/s (SP-4203), which is the same.
  The ground asked for 48 ft/s in 1 min 14 s. The press kit's plan was 66 ft/s (20 m/s) at 1:30.
- **When the instruments' power supply failed.** "About 20 minutes into the first orbit"
  (SP-4203, p. 235); the transcript has the first word of it at 23 min 35 s and "Lost a primary
  converter" 39 s later, which fits the history's 45 seconds from first notice to the switch.
- **The fairings.** 3 min 15 s in the press kit's plan; after the second stage's cutoff in
  McDonnell's manual for this ship; between 6:15 and 6:21 in the transcript. See "Timeline".
- **The retrofire.** 4:33:23, 16,403 s (the guidance reconstruction; SP-4002; the transcript);
  planned for 4:33:30 (press kit).
- **The vehicle's height.** 1,297.118 in by the stations (108 ft 1 in); "109 feet" (press kit);
  "108 ft" (the launch systems report's simplified figure); "approximately 110 feet" (SP-121).
- **The first stage engines' exit plane.** Station 1348.103 (the launch systems report) or 1342.31
  (the Gemini IV mission report's figure).
- **The photograph NASA captions as Gemini-Titan 3 on the pad, S65-10461,** is dated November 1964
  in the same library and shows the number 12557 on the vehicle: it is taken as GLV-2.

## Not found

- **The mission report itself**, *Gemini Program Mission Report GT-3, Gemini 3* (MSC-G-R-65-2):
  not on NTRS under any search tried, nor at archive.org, ibiblio or NASA's transcript pages. Its
  guidance reconstruction (supplemental report 4) and the voice transcript are, and are what the

  vehicles before them.

  derived (see "Timeline").
- **The weather where it landed**, as observed: only what the crew were told an hour and three
  quarters before.
- **What stood in the rendezvous and recovery section in the radar's place.**
- **The vehicle's number as painted on GLV-3**, and anything on the far side of the vehicle from
  the two photographs.

## Where the spacecraft is now

At the Grissom Memorial in Spring Mill State Park, near Mitchell, Indiana. One photograph of it
there has been read (Wikimedia Commons, *Gemini3 Capsule.jpg*, 2011): one hatch open, the
opening's red seal, the hatch's inner face with its window ring, a seat's headrest, the forward
shingles bare metal. The model has not been compared with the ship as it is kept.
