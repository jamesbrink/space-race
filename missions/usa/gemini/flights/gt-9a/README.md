# Gemini-Titan 9A (Gemini IX-A)

Three rendezvous in a day with a target that could not be docked with, because the shroud over its
docking cone had not come off; then two hours outside, in which Eugene Cernan found out how hard it
is to work with nothing to push against, and did not fly the backpack he had gone out to fly. The
crew were the backups: Elliot See and Charles Bassett, who were to have flown it, died on 28
February 1966 when their T-38 struck the building in St. Louis where this spacecraft was being
built.

**In this repo: the vehicle, in the gallery, and its target beside it.** Spacecraft No. 9 on GLV-9
as they left the pad can be turned, taken apart as they staged, and cut open: the covers over its
hatch windows, the docking fittings it never used, the handrails, the gold inside of its adapter
and the Astronaut Maneuvering Unit at the back of it; the right hatch opens at the end of the
slider. The target stands on the floor beside the launch vehicle, to scale, as the crew found it:
the two halves of its shroud open about thirty degrees and held by a band and two wires. It can be
stood alone (`#usa/gemini/gt-9a/gallery/docking-target`), taken apart and cut open. At Complex 19 the
vehicle stands on the thrust mount on the morning of the flight, without the target, which went up
from Complex 14 two days before: the erector raised while the crew go up and laid down from T−55,
Stafford and Cernan on the approach to the ramp, Cernan in the cover for going outside with its legs of
Chromel-R, each a figure to walk the site as, under that morning's sky. Complex 14 and the flight
itself are not built.

## The name

The flight is `usa/gemini/gt-9a`, by its designation; its `callsign` is the mission's own style,
Gemini IX-A. Gemini IX was the mission as planned, with an Agena; when that Agena was lost on 17 May
1966 the flight with the substitute target became IX-A, as Gemini VI had become VI-A.

## How the target is shown

As Gemini-Titan 8's is ([its README](../gt-8/README.md), "How the target is shown"): the gallery
shows one model of a flight, so the target is part of the vehicle's model under its own heading,
"Docking target", 20 ft (6.1 m) from the Titan's axis. It stands on its tail, on the tip of its
stub antenna, which nothing could: **an exhibit's arrangement** of a thing that was only ever on
top of an Atlas or in orbit. The Atlas is not built.

## What happened

**Three targets.** Agena 5004 went into the Atlantic on 17 May when one of its Atlas's booster
engines swung hard over and stayed there (*On the Shoulders of Titans*, p. 330). The ATDA, built
that winter against just this, went up on 1 June at 15:00:02.363 G.m.t. into an orbit of 158.5 by
161.5 nautical miles, and its telemetry said the shroud might not have gone. The spacecraft's own
count that day stopped at T−3 minutes: the last update of the launch azimuth could not be sent to
the ship's computer. Two days later it went, at 13:39:33.335 G.m.t. on an azimuth of 87.4°.

**The first rendezvous** was the quickest yet, in the third revolution: 74.8 ft/s at 0:49:03, 14.8
at 1:55:17, 53.4 at 2:24:52 to make the orbit coelliptic, and the terminal burn of 28.4 ft/s at
3:36:02. The radar held the target from 120 nautical miles. Braking began at 4:06:08; by 4:15 the
ship was 60 ft from the ATDA, in the dark. "The shroud is half open on that thing!" As the Sun came
up Stafford gave it its name: "It looks like an angry alligator out here rotating around" (*Titans*,
p. 333). The pyrotechnic bolts had fired and the band had parted, but the lanyards that should have
pulled the connectors apart had been taped down, and the wires held the two halves by their ends
(mission report, 5.8.1.2). Stafford wanted to nudge it open with the docking bar; the ground would
not have it. There was to be no docking.

**The second and third.** At 5:01 the ship went 20 ft/s straight up out of the target's orbit, to
come back to it one revolution later using no radar and no computer, only the sextant and the
charts: station keeping again from 6:36 to 7:14:58. Then it backed away, slept, and next morning
came down on the target from above and ahead, as an Apollo command module would have to come down
on a lunar module stranded low: 14.4 ft/s at 19:54:24, 18.0 at 21:02:28, station keeping from 21:42,
at times within inches. Against the ground below, the crew lost sight of the target for a while,
and without the radar would not have found it. At 22:59 they left it for good.

**Outside.** The walk was put off a day; the crew were tired. Cernan opened his hatch at 49:23 and
stood up, took in the S-12 collector at 49:27, put up a mirror and a camera, and went forward along
the nose on his umbilical. Nothing held him: the Velcro was not strong enough, the umbilical led him
about, and every move cost more work than it had on the ground. He went aft over the adapter's edge
at 50:09, in the dark, to the unit at the back. Unfolding its arms and plugging himself into it took
far longer and far more effort than planned; about eight minutes after sunset his visor began to
fog, and the suit's cooling could not keep up. He backed into the unit at 50:39 and changed over to
its oxygen and its radio, which was poor. He could see very little. At 51:03 Stafford called the
unit no-go. Cernan unhooked himself, went back along the handrails, and with Stafford holding his
legs so that he could rest, and then helping him in, the two of them closed the hatch about 51:28
(mission report, 7.1.2; *Titans*, pp. 338 to 340; NASA SP-149). The unit stayed where it hung.

**Home.** Retrofire was at 71:46:44 in the forty-fifth revolution; the ship's computer flew the
re-entry, and at drogue deployment it was 0.38 nautical mile from the point aimed at. The *Wasp*
watched it land.

## The morning at the pad

By the clocks at the Cape. "The crew ingressed at T-115 minutes, and the subsequent tasks and
checkout progressed smoothly to the scheduled hold at T-3 minutes" (the mission report, 7.1.2.1);
the count's zero was "the nominal 13:39:30 G.m.t." (5.2.10.3). How long the hold at T-3 lasted is
not in what was read, and without it no minute of the count can be put on the clock: Gemini 8's
scheduled six minutes are taken. So every time below is `estimated`; the minutes of the count are
the record's for the crew and the press kit's plan (release 66-97, pp. 6 and 7) for the rest. The
pad's slider plays it ([pad.ts](pad.ts)).

| --- | --- | --- |
| about 6:38 | T-115: in the spacecraft | estimated |
| about 6:53 | T-100: the hatches shut (NASA S66-34065 has the technicians about to close them) | estimated |
| about 7:23 | T-70: the white room empty | estimated |
| about 7:38 | T-55: the erector starts down | estimated |
| about 8:30 to 8:36 | the built-in hold at T-3; the computer's update did not come, and a backup plan let the count go on | estimated |
| 8:39:33.335 | liftoff, at the second for the rendezvous | documented |

It was the third time up the erector: on 17 May the count stopped at T-85 when the Atlas carrying the
Agena failed, and on 1 June, the target in orbit, at T-3, the ground unable to give the ship's
computer its update; each time the crew came down in the elevator.

**What stood there.** The complex as the flights before left it ([lc-19.md](../../../places/cape-canaveral/lc-19.md),
"What stood when"): after Gemini 8 the umbilical tower's drop-weight lanyards were given new lengths,
and this time the spacecraft's upper umbilical dropped as it should (5.2.10.3). That is not modelled.

## The weather

**The mission report's** (MSC-G-R-66-6, 12.2, p. 12-13), "at 8:40 a.m. e.s.t.": "Scattered clouds,
3500 feet; stratocumulus, 2/10 covered; haze aloft", the wind 080° at 16 knots, visibility 10 miles,
30.23 in. Hg, 77 °F with a dew point of 58 °F, 52 per cent humidity. On 1 June it had been cloudier,
four layers up to an overcast at 20,000 ft and 7 miles.

## What is not built

- **Of the launch site** ([lc-19.md](../../../places/cape-canaveral/lc-19.md), which lists it all): the people of
  the morning but the two who flew; the crew's van; the ventilator each man carried and his yellow
  overshoes. Every time of the morning is estimated.
- **Complex 14** and the Atlas that carried the target (issue #34): at the site the target is not drawn.
- **The crew in their seats.** Stafford and Cernan stand on the approach to the ramp in the G4C
  ([g4c-suit.md](../../crew/g4c-suit.md)), Cernan's let out over its cover for going outside and its legs
  of Chromel-R; his overvisor is not built, and whether it was on his helmet at the pad is not
  settled. Cernan's stature is not in any NASA source read: 6 ft is taken. The seats are empty.
- **The flight.** No trajectory, no orbit as data, no simulator run.
- **Cernan's two hours.** The unit, the bars and the rail are as they were at launch.

## Where the spacecraft is now

At the Kennedy Space Center Visitor Complex, by general knowledge; no record of it has been read,
and the flown ship has not been compared with the model.
