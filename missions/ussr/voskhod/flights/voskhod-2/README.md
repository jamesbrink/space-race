# Voskhod 2

**18 to 19 March 1965 · Pavel Belyayev and Aleksey Leonov · callsign Алмаз (Almaz, "Diamond") · Voskhod 3KD No. 4 · Voskhod 11A57 No. R15000-05 · Site 1, Tyuratam**

The first walk in space. An hour and a half after liftoff Leonov went out through an airlock made
of cloth and rubber, floated on a line for twelve minutes, and got back in only by letting pressure
out of his suit. A day later the automatic descent failed, Belyayev flew the ship down by hand one
orbit late, and it came down in deep forest north of Perm, where the two spent two nights in the
snow before they could be got out. Backups: Dmitriy Zaikin and Yevgeniy Khrunov.

## What happened

All times are Moscow time (UTC+3). They are from two documents of 1965 unless the text says
otherwise: the timetable in the file of the flight's records, signed by the sports commissars (as
Borisenko's book prints it; "the record's file" below), and OKB-1's preliminary technical report,
signed by Korolev on 6 June. The crew's own words are from their written report and from the
transcript of the State Commission's session of 22 March, both published by Roscosmos in 2020.

The ship was the Voskhod with one couch taken out and an airlock put on. A hard airlock would not go
under the fairing, so the suit makers at Tomilino built a soft one, the Volga: a tube with walls of
rubber and cloth, folded to 0.7 m for launch, that stood out 2.5 m when forty air beams in its
walls were filled. The same works made the suit, the Berkut, with a pack of oxygen for 45 minutes.
A test ship, Kosmos 57, went up on 22 February and worked its airlock, and then two ground stations
sent it the same command at the same moment, which it read as an order to descend; it fired its
engine pointing the wrong way and blew itself up on its third orbit. So nobody had seen the airlock
shot off, or a ball come home with the airlock's ring on its side. The ring was flown on a
reconnaissance satellite instead, which landed safely on 15 March.

On the 18th the two were woken at 03:30, suited by 07:00 and aboard by about 07:30, Leonov first
(the crew's report, whose times are faint in the copy read).
Liftoff was at 10:00:00. Belyayev counted the stages: the strap-ons at 118 seconds, the fairing at
153, the core at 292, the third stage's cutoff at 523. The orbit was 173 by 495 km, higher than
asked.

Belyayev let the airlock out at 10:22:54 and its beams filled to 0.6 atmospheres. He helped Leonov
on with the pack; Leonov went through the hatch into the airlock, catching first the pack and then
the release of his still camera on the way, and made his line fast at three points. Belyayev shut
the hatch behind him and let the air out: the airlock was empty at 11:28:13 and its outer hatch was
fully open at 11:32:54. Leonov kept asking to go. "Wait, Lyosha, there will be time." At 11:34:51,
coming up to the Black Sea, he was out. He took the cap off the cine camera on the airlock's rim
and threw it away, pushed off to the length of his line, 5.35 m at the furthest, and hung there
turning slowly. Once he pushed off too hard and came back at the ship fast enough to fear for his
helmet. He could see the Kerch peninsula, the bay at Novorossiysk and the Caucasus, and later the
Volga, the Urals, the Ob and the Yenisei. Belyayev heard every push through the hull and watched
him on a screen. The still camera on his suit could not be worked: its release had been torn off
in the hatch.

At the Yenisei he was ordered in. The first try failed: the suit was stiff, and he had been
fighting it, he said afterwards, for some twenty minutes. He had thought on the ground of what he
would do, told Belyayev he was going to the second setting, and let the pressure down from 0.4 to
0.27 atmospheres with his left hand while he held on with his right. He unshipped the cine camera,
got both feet into the airlock with his stomach against its rim, pushed the camera in ahead of him
with his right hand and followed it. He was inside at 11:47:00, twelve minutes and nine seconds
after he came out; the hatch was shut at 11:48:40 and the air let back in from 11:51:54. With the
suit soft again he turned round in the airlock to stow the cameras, and came into the cabin feet
first. He was sweating heavily, and with Belyayev's agreement opened his helmet. This is what the
two of them told the State Commission four days later. In the accounts Leonov gave from the 1990s
on, he went in head first against the plan, turned in the airlock while the suit was still hard,
and told nobody that he was letting his pressure down.

The airlock was shot off some two and a quarter hours into the flight, "with a sharp sound, like a
balloon bursting", and left the ship turning once in 20 to 22 seconds, which the State Commission
chose to leave alone. The oxygen in the cabin rose through the night, to 355 mm of mercury by
Belyayev's gauge, and at some point a switch knocked by the suits' hoses let an air bottle into the
cabin until the pressure reached 860 mm; it was found and shut at 05:50:53.

On the 16th orbit the automatic descent was to begin. The commands went through; the ship went on
turning; the Sun-seeker did not hold, and the engine did not fire. Belyayev asked to land by hand.
The numbers were worked out on the ground and read up to him by Gagarin. The Vzor was at the crew's
left and across the way they lay, so Leonov got out of his couch and down beside it, and Belyayev
took off his helmet and lay across both couches to see through it. He took the ship by hand at
11:19:00 and turned it through nearly half a circle. At 11:36:27 he pressed the button, with
neither of them yet back in place; the ship began to swing as the engine lit, and he pulled it back
with the hand controller. The engine ran for about 39 seconds. The instrument module did not come
away for some twelve to fourteen minutes more. The load was about 7 g. The landing system came on
at 11:56:08 and they were down at 12:02:17, in forest, 368 km beyond where they should have been.

A birch lay across the hatch. The snow was a metre and a half deep and the trees 25 to 30 m tall.
The aerial did not work until they had hung it up on parachute lines, and it was three hours before
the control post knew where they were. A civil helicopter found them at about 15:50 and warm
clothes were dropped three quarters of an hour later; nothing could land. They spent the night
there. The next morning three men were put down by helicopter 1.5 km away and reached them on skis
at 11:35. A marshal would not have them hoisted from a hovering helicopter, so a landing place was
made ready 1.7 km off and they spent a second night, with tents and food this time. On the 21st
they skied out to it, were lifted to Perm by 09:50 and were at Tyuratam by 17:30. At the press
conference of 26 March the landing in the forest was not owned to.

## The weather

No figure for the morning of 18 March was found, and the two accounts read disagree. Chertok
(vol. 3, p. 263): "the weather was lousy. There was a low cloud cover and intermittent drizzling
rain", and the rocket left "a perceptible hole in the blanket of clouds". Siddiqi (p. 454): "It was
a cold and snowy day at Tyura-Tam." Two days earlier Kamanin has "a still, sunny morning ... minus
8". Over the Black Sea as Leonov looked down the weather was "clear, cloudless, sunny". At the
landing place: snow a metre and a half deep, about −5 °C (Kamanin, 19 March), and on the 21st
cloud at 600 m with 10 km of visibility.

## Where the sources disagree

- **How Leonov got back in.** Feet first, with the cine camera pushed in ahead, at the suit's lower
  pressure, which he had planned for on the ground and reported as he did it (his report to the
  State Commission, 22 March 1965; the crew's written report). Head first, against the plan, having
  told nobody about the pressure, and turning round inside a hard suit with his pulse at 190 (his
  article of 2005, *Two Sides of the Moon*, and interviews; Siddiqi, p. 456, follows these). Zak
  reports that the airlock's own film, released in 2019 and 2020, shows him coming in feet first.
  **The record of 1965 is taken here**, and the later account is given as his.
- **His hands and feet coming out of the gloves and boots**, the heat stroke and the litres of
  sweat are in every later account and in neither document of 1965, where he says he "did not feel
  heating over the whole surface of the body".
- **How long he was outside.** 12 min 9 s from showing to hiding (the record's file; Chertok);
  "about 10 minutes" (OKB-1; TASS); 16 minutes with the hatch open (OKB-1); 23 min 41 s in vacuum
  (the record's file); "some 20 minutes" fighting the suit (Leonov, 1965). Siddiqi gives 11:51:54
  as the hatch's closing, which the file has as the start of refilling.
- **The tether.** 5.35 m is the furthest he went (the record's file); Abramov and Skoog describe a
  7 m umbilical; Siddiqi has "five-meter cord" and "5.35-meter-long tether".
- **The oxygen.** 355 mm Hg at most (Belyayev); "more than 360" for several orbits (OKB-1); 45 per
  cent (Kamanin); 460 mm (Leonov, 2005). OKB-1 puts it to the regenerator and the airlock's air.
  **A hatch that did not seal** is in Leonov's later accounts and in Siddiqi, and not in the report.
- **The air bottle.** 140 to 25 atm (Belyayev) or 75 to 25 over the 13th orbit (Kamanin).
- **The spin after the airlock went.** A turn in 20 to 22 s, which is 17° a second (Belyayev); 20°
  a second (Kamanin; Siddiqi); pitch and yaw up to 4.5° a second and roll over 6 (OKB-1's report,
  from telemetry).
- **Who was where when the engine fired.** Both out of their couches, and the button pressed first
  (Belyayev, 22 March). Back in their couches first, taking 46 seconds, which made the overshoot
  (Kamanin, 22 April; Siddiqi; Chertok).
- **The overshoot.** 368 km (Kamanin; Chertok); 386 (Siddiqi; spacefacts.de); "more than 800"
  (RussianSpaceWeb); 80 km from a place the crew chose (Leonov, 2005).
- **Where it landed.** 180 km north by north-west of Perm, with coordinates (the record's file);
  "northwest" (Siddiqi); near Shchuchino, 25 to 30 km south-west of Berezniki, and elsewhere "70 to
  80 km from the regional centre" (Kamanin).
- **The load.** About 7 g (Belyayev, 1965); 10 g (Leonov, later).
- **The night.** −5 °C (Kamanin); below −30 °C (Leonov, 2004). Wolves and bears are in the later
  accounts only.
- **Who forbade the hoist.** Rudenko (Kamanin; Chertok) or Vershinin (Siddiqi).
- **The orbit count** (17 or 18), **the mass** (5,682, 5,685 or McDowell's 5,825 kg), **the
  orbit** (Siddiqi's table and RussianSpaceWeb print "137" for the perigee; Wikipedia 167 × 475).
- **The weather at launch**, as above.
