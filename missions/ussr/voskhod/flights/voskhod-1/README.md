# Voskhod 1

**12 to 13 October 1964 · Vladimir Komarov, Konstantin Feoktistov, Boris Yegorov · callsign Рубин (Rubin, "Ruby") · Voskhod 3KV No. 3 · Voskhod 11A57 No. R15000-04 · Site 1, Tyuratam**

The first crew of more than one: a pilot, an engineer and a doctor in a Vostok's ball with its
ejection seat taken out and three couches put in across it. They flew without pressure suits, on a
launch vehicle with no escape system, and were the first to land inside their ship. Sixteen orbits
in one day, seventeen minutes and three seconds. The man who had ordered the flight spoke to them
on the third orbit and was out of office before they got back to Moscow. Backups: Boris Volynov,
Georgiy Katys, Aleksey Sorokin and Vasiliy Lazarev.

## What happened

All times are Moscow time (UTC+3) unless they say otherwise. Kamanin's diary keeps the range's
clock at the pad, two hours ahead of Moscow, and says so: his times there are converted here.

The ship came out of a decision of early 1964 to fly three men at once before the Americans flew
two. There was no new spacecraft to do it with, so OKB-1 altered the Vostok: the draft plan was
finished in August, and what it removed was the ejection seat with its parachute and survival kit,
the pressure suit and its air conditioning, the film camera and the biological experiments. In went
three couches, a reserve braking engine, a second main parachute with a rocket in its lines, an
orientation system that worked in the dark, a new radio and television. The escape tower meant for
the next ship was not ready, and the flight went without one. The plan itself said rescue would be
"difficult" for the first 25 to 44 seconds.

The whole landing system was dropped once from an aircraft at Feodosiya on 6 September, in the ball
Titov had flown, and the parachute hatch did not blow: the ball was destroyed. More drops followed
and worked, the last of them watched by Korolev from a helicopter. On 6 October a ship of the same
kind, 3KV No. 2, went up with dummies as Kosmos 47, flew a day and came down whole, dragged 160 m
by the wind; its third stage's thrust had sagged by a tenth for three seconds, for no reason anyone
found before the crew flew.

The crew had been argued over since the spring. Komarov was a cosmonaut of the first group.
Feoktistov was one of the ship's own designers and Yegorov a doctor, both on a much shortened
training.
On 9 October the State Commission confirmed them and set the launch for 10:30 on the 12th; the same
day they lay in their couches in the assembly building while the Commission's members climbed to
the hatch in pairs to look. "Very cramped," Kamanin wrote, "their poses are uncomfortable, it is
hard to change position, there is perhaps a fifth of the living room there was in the Vostoks."

The 12th was still and frosty. Without suits to put on, the morning was short: up at 06:30 by the
range's clock, breakfast, sensors, and ready by nine. The bus reached the pad at 08:15 Moscow time.
Komarov reported to the chairman as an engineer lieutenant colonel; they rode up in the lift,
Feoktistov and Yegorov first; at the top they took off their jackets and boots, put on slippers
and went in, Yegorov first, then Feoktistov, Komarov last, and by 08:35 they were checking the
ship. Korolev, by Kamanin's account, was shaking. Liftoff was at 10:30:01. "An agonising 523
seconds," Kamanin wrote, "and a sigh of relief: the ship was in orbit."

The orbit was 177.5 by 408 km. An hour and a half after liftoff the control post saw the crew by
television and spoke to them; on the third orbit Khrushchev and Mikoyan, on holiday at Pitsunda,
spoke to them by radio. On the sixth orbit Komarov turned the ship by hand against the stars, and
Feoktistov took star sights; on the sixth and seventh Komarov ran the ion engines fitted outside.
Yegorov took blood, measured blood pressure and tested reflexes. Feoktistov and Yegorov were both
disoriented within two or three hours, and stayed so: Feoktistov felt he was upside down, Yegorov
that he was kneeling head down. On the seventh orbit Yegorov's pulse read 46 on the telemetry while
he slept; Komarov took it by hand, and it was 68. The cabin warmed from 15 °C to 21 °C over six
orbits. Voice by VHF was good, short wave was poor, and from the eighth orbit to the thirteenth,
when the ship did not pass over the Soviet Union, nothing was heard at all. Contact came back at
04:28 on the 13th.

At about 08:00 Korolev asked Khrushchev's leave to land, and got it. About an hour later, when he
asked the crew whether they were ready, Komarov answered that they would like to go on: they had
seen many interesting things. The press of the day printed Korolev's reply as a line of *Hamlet*
(«Много есть, о друг Горацио, чудес на свете») followed by "We shall work to the programme".
The flight was planned for one day and the stores and air would have stretched to two or three.

The descent was heard badly. The ship turned itself at 09:55:39 and fired its braking engine over
the Gulf of Guinea at 10:18:58 (by the one account that gives seconds); ships off Africa reported
the engine's start and stop late. At 10:22:30 Moscow heard the ball's own beacon, which meant the
engine had run and the ball had parted from the instrument module. Direction finders followed it
over the Caspian and the Aral Sea. Then the report that the parachute hatch had blown, and nothing:
the aerial in the parachute lines was silent. A search pilot, Mikhaylov in an Il-14, saw it in the
air 40 km east of Maryevka. Korolev took the microphone from the operator: one parachute or two?
Two. Minutes later the same pilot saw the ball on the ground and three people beside it, waving.
It was 10:47, 312 km north-east of Kustanay.

They were flown by helicopter to Kokchetav and on to Kustanay, where a telephone call with
Khrushchev had been arranged. Word came from Moscow not to wait for it. They reached Tyuratam that
evening and reported to some two hundred people the next morning. That night the Central Committee
met; on the morning of the 15th it was announced that Khrushchev had been relieved. The crew were
received in Moscow on the 19th by his successors.

## The weather

For the launch, Kamanin's diary of 12 October: «Тихое морозное утро, температура — минус 8
градусов ... Метеоусловия для старта идеальные: ветер — 3–4 метра в секунду, видимость — более
20 километров, высокие перистые облака.» A still, frosty morning, −8 °C at six by the range's
clock; wind 3 to 4 m/s, visibility over 20 km, high cirrus. Chertok has the night clear and still
and down to −10 °C; TASS's correspondent has the pad "flooded with a sparing autumn sun". No wind
direction and no cloud height were found, and no station's report was looked for. For the landing
there is only the forecast of the 11th for both places, "clear, wind 5 m/s". Six days earlier, for
Kosmos 47: seven tenths of cloud, wind 8 to 10 m/s gusting to 15, 9 to 12 °C.

## Where the sources disagree

- **The orbit.** 178 by 409 km and 90.1 min (TASS) against 177.5 by 408 (Siddiqi; the Soviet
  encyclopedia). A period of 90.1 min belongs to an orbit some 20 km lower at apogee than either.
  Later catalogues give 336 to 377 km for the apogee (McDowell 371; Orbital Focus 377; Wikipedia
  and Encyclopedia Astronautica 178 × 336 at 64.7°): later epochs, or other fits. The inclination
  is 64.9° in Siddiqi's text and 64.77° in his Table IB, which is also Kosmos 47's figure.
- **When the braking engine fired.** 10:18:58 (RussianSpaceWeb) or 10:08:56 (Chertok, as the time
  in the sequencer's programme). The beacon heard at 10:22:30 and the landing at 10:47 fit the
  first.
- **Where it landed.** 312 km north-east of Kustanay (the encyclopedia; Siddiqi). spacefacts.de
  prints "northwest" beside coordinates of 54°02′ N 68°08′ E, which are north-east; English
  Wikipedia has 52°02′ N. Kamanin has it seen 40 km east of Maryevka and down 60 km east of it.
  No survey was found.
- **How long escape was impossible.** To the 25th to 44th second "difficult" (OKB-1's plan, in
  Siddiqi); to the 27th "in fact impossible", then to the 44th "difficult but possible" (Kamanin,
  14 September); to the 20th (Kamanin, 22 August); "the first minute or so" (Siddiqi, p. 423).
- **The Hamlet line.** Printed in 1964 and in Golovanov; left unfinished in other tellings; said
  by Feoktistov and not Komarov to have been provoked, in another; and absent from Chertok, whose
  Korolev says only "But that wasn't the agreement!"
- **The margin.** Three days of resources (Kamanin, 13 October); two (Chertok). Siddiqi has the
  engineers allowing "at most one full day of safety" for the leak.
- **How soft the landing was.** "Soft" at the press conference of 21 October. Feoktistov, later:
  sparks from their eyes, and the ball rolled over and left them hanging in their straps.
- **Kosmos 47's dummies.** Two (Kamanin) or three (Siddiqi).
- **The suits' colour.** Grey (Kamanin; Siddiqi) or "dark" (TASS's correspondent).
- **The couches' travel.** 200 to 300 mm (Siddiqi) or 20 to 40 mm (Kamanin).
- **The mass.** 5,320 kg (Siddiqi) against Chertok's "5.7 metric tons".
- **The launch time.** Russian Wikipedia's box has 07:26:00 UTC from NSSDC; every other source
  read has 10:30 Moscow time, and those that give seconds, 10:30:01.
