# Vostok 5

**14 to 19 June 1963 · Valeriy Bykovskiy · callsign Ястреб (Yastreb, "Hawk") · Vostok 3KA No. 7 · Vostok-K 8K72K No. E103-24 · Site 1, Tyuratam**

Five days alone in orbit: 4 days, 23 hours and 7 minutes to his landing, which is still the longest
anyone has flown by himself. It was meant to be eight. The second group flight:
[Vostok 6](../vostok-6/README.md), with the first woman to fly in space, left the same pad 45 hours
31 minutes later, and for the first of their three days together the two talked by radio. It ended
as every Vostok did, with the pilot ejecting from the descent module and landing by parachute.
Backup: Boris Volynov.

## What happened

All times are Moscow time (UTC+3) unless they say otherwise. Kamanin's diary keeps the range's
clock, two hours ahead of Moscow, and says so only now and then: his times are converted here.

Two ships were left of the batch, No. 7 and No. 8, and through 1962 both were meant for women:
Alekseev's works had made two women's seats. The military would have flown one and sent the other
to a museum. What was settled in March and April 1963 was a man for up to eight days and a woman
for up to three, in orbit together. One of the two seats was balanced again for Bykovskiy, who
weighed 90.7 kg in his suit; it could not then be made to suit Volynov at 104.5 kg, and the ship,
stocked for eight days with air for ten, had no weight to spare. Volynov stayed the backup all the
same. On 11 May the chief designers reported on ships No. 7 and No. 8: 28 instruments had failed in
test and been changed, and a stray washer had been found in a distribution box, "again, as in
1962". Ships and rockets had by then waited more than a year.

The cosmonauts flew to Tyuratam on 1 June. On the 4th the State Commission named Bykovskiy for
Vostok 5 and Tereshkova for Vostok 6. Rollout was set for the morning of the 6th and put off when
the command radio link's receiver failed (a triode, an open lead); the rocket went to the pad at
07:00 on the 9th, and on the 10th Bykovskiy sat in the ship while Korolev talked to him through
the open hatch. That night the launch set for the 11th was called off, the first time one had
been: the Crimean observatory warned of flares on the Sun, and a dose of more than 50 roentgens
could not be ruled out for three or four days. Korolev, angry at the caution, said the rocket could
stand on the pad for two weeks. Moscow gave its leave on the 13th.

On the 14th the State Commission met at the pad at 06:00 and set the launch for 12:00. The bus
reached the pad two and a quarter hours before that. With Bykovskiy aboard, both VHF transmitters
were found dead: he could hear the ground on six channels and be heard on three, all short wave.
They decided to fly. Forty minutes before the time a fitter drawing the ejection seat's safety pin
found its lanyard jammed and cut it with a knife; the hatch was closed over the stub; he told his
mates on the ground, who raised the alarm, and the hatch came off again: half an hour. Five minutes
before the new time the lamp that confirms the third stage ready did not light. A gyroscope of its
guidance had failed (Chertok), and it was changed on the fuelled rocket with the pilot aboard. The
window closed at 15:00, and to stand down meant draining the rocket and sending it back to the
works until August, by when the two ships would have been past their guaranteed life. Marshal
Krylov advised it. Korolev, Tyulin, Kirillov and Pilyugin did not. In the last seconds the cable
mast was seen not to have let go; they let the count run, and the cable tore out as the rocket
left (Golovanov, as Siddiqi has him). Vostok 5 lifted off at 14:58:58, at the end of its window,
three hours late. "The launch was at 14:59," Bykovskiy told the Commission, "and the radio
announced 15:00."

He heard the engines only faintly, hardly noticed the strap-ons go, did not feel the third stage
light, and heard a bang as it shut down; at separation he saw snowflakes. In orbit the VHF
transmitters worked. TASS announced the orbit that had been asked for, 181 by 235 km. At 22:12,
after five orbits, it gave the one that had been reached: 175 by 222. On the fourth orbit
Khrushchev spoke to him; on the sixth the control post saw him on television, serious and a little
tired, and Kamanin asked for a smile and got one. The first reckoning gave the orbit ten or eleven
days of life. By the 17th it was plain that it was coming down faster than that: a seven-day flight
was "practically excluded", and Kamanin ordered him to hold the cabin, which had gone from 30 °C to
15 in the first day, at 10, in case a lower orbit should cool it further.

He did what the programme asked by orbits, not by the clock. Ten minutes of orienting by hand took
10 atmospheres off the bottles, and the ship followed the stick easily; set rolling to the right,
it turned once in eight minutes by the stars and the Moon. He undid his harness as Nikolayev and
Popovich had taught him and floated on his 18th, 34th, 50th and 66th orbits, a whole orbit at a
time: his head went up first and he stuck to the ceiling by his stomach, and he looked forward to
the next. Through a porthole he saw an antenna and a loose piece of the ship's covering flapping.
He filmed the horizon, the Moon and the Earth in black and white, though the camera's cassette went
in badly and jammed and one cassette had no film in it; the binoculars gave nothing, being hard to
focus, and he looked at the Earth through the camera instead. He watched peas sprout. On the
evening of the 15th he stopped answering two orbits before his sleep was due; the television showed
him with his eyes shut and the telemetry a pulse of 54.

Vostok 6 went up on the 16th, and he heard Chayka as soon as she was in orbit: "she is singing me
songs". The two ships were about five kilometres apart at their nearest, just after she reached
orbit, in planes some thirty degrees apart; he looked for her and did not see her. Their voice
link was excellent the first day, worse the second and gone the third.

At 09:05 on the 18th Khabarovsk took down a short-wave message from Yastreb: "there was a cosmic
knock". Korolev had Kamanin find out what had struck the ship, and somebody was set to work out how
big a meteorite a man would hear. Asked what kind of knock, Bykovskiy did not know what was meant,
then laughed: not *stuk* but *stul*, a stool. He had taken a laxative on the third day and used the
ship's sanitary device for the purpose nobody had yet used one for in orbit. The control post
congratulated him on the record. At 14:34 a telegram told him he had been admitted to the Party.
That evening it was decided: Vostok 6 down on her 49th orbit, Vostok 5 on his 82nd, both on the
19th.

On the 19th Gagarin told him: "Chayka has landed, now it is your turn." He saw the solar
orientation take hold; the braking engine lit smoothly and ran 39 seconds. Until the two halves of
the ship parted the ball tumbled fast and without order; the loads after that were not great. The
hatch went with a loud bang and two seconds later the seat left smoothly. He came down at 14:06 in
steppe between two villages, 540 km north-west of Karaganda; a horseman reached him first, then a
car, and by the time his suit was off a hundred people stood round him, so that he could not raise
the search aircraft overhead by radio. The ball lay a kilometre and a half or two away. Both ships
had come down two degrees of latitude north of where they were aimed, and the control post had no
word of either pilot for hours.

"As printed since" is Siddiqi's text (p. 368) and Table IB, with the period RussianSpaceWeb's table
gives from the Soviet encyclopedia. The first announcement, made as he reached orbit, is the orbit
that had been asked for: Vostok 6 was given almost the same figures two days later and kept them.

**No OKB-1 report of this flight has been read.** `flight.ts` quotes 174.7, 222.1, 64.96° and 88.27.

## Where the sources disagree

- **Why the orbit was low.** Siddiqi: "evidently because of the less-than-nominal performance by
  the third stage", with no source. *Aviation Week* (Klass, 24 June 1963) argued from the two sets
  of TASS figures that the ship had parted from the stage early; a week later it had American
  tracking put the spent stage some 20 km higher at apogee than the ship. Kamanin wrote on the day
  that the ship had "reached the calculated orbit, with a life of up to eleven days", and neither
  he nor Chertok says what went short. Bykovskiy heard the stage stop and felt nothing odd.
- **How long the orbit would last.** Eleven days (Kamanin, 14 June); ten to eleven, "guaranteed"
  (Chertok); about eight, against ten for the other ships (Siddiqi). RussianSpaceWeb gives this
  ship Kamanin's "7.1 days", which in the diary is Vostok 6's.
- **The second of liftoff.** 14:58:58 (Siddiqi's text; the FAI's file; McDowell) or 14:58:59
  (Borisenko, the sports commissioner who kept the record; spacefacts.de; RussianSpaceWeb). Siddiqi's
  launch log prints "1158:58" in a column headed Moscow Time: it is UTC.
- **The time set, and the hold.** Noon Moscow time and three hours late (Kamanin, at the pad;
  Chertok: "an additional 3 hours"), or 09:00 Moscow time and "a full six hours" (Siddiqi, pp. 366
  and 368).
- **What stopped the count five minutes before.** The lamp for Block E on the bunker's board
  (Kamanin); a failed gyro horizon in the third stage (Chertok). The two fit together; Kamanin does
  not name the cause, nor that anything was changed.
- **What put off the rollout of 6 June.** The command radio link's receiver (Kamanin, who was
  there); the wind (Chertok, who was in Moscow).
- **The landing.** 14:06 (TASS; the FAI's file as 11:06 GMT) or 14:03 (Borisenko). 4 d 23 h 6 min
  in Siddiqi's text and in TASS's "119 hours 6 minutes"; 04:23:07:02 in his Table IB, which is the
  announced minute less 14:58:58.
- **What 118 h 56 min 41 s measures.** It is the FAI's record, still standing, for a flight by one
  man. Siddiqi, from Glushko's encyclopedia, calls it the descent module's time. Borisenko's
  notebook for Vostok 3 stops the clock when the pilot "separated from the ship". On that rule
  Bykovskiy left the ball at 13:55:39 and was ten minutes under his parachute, which is about what
  a descent from seven kilometres takes; on the other the ball landed ten minutes before he did,
  which is also what Vostok 3's did. Nothing read settles it.
- **How many orbits.** 81 (TASS; Siddiqi) or 82 (English Wikipedia). He landed on the 82nd.
- **Where he landed.** 53°23′45″ N 67°36′41″ E (spacefacts.de; McDowell agrees to a hundredth of a
  degree); the monument near Karatal is at 53.398° N 67.605° E. Russian Wikipedia's header has
  68°37′ E, which looks like a slip.
- **Whether the modules parted late.** Siddiqi: "as with Gagarin and Titov, his instrument
  compartment failed to separate on time". The report as Kamanin wrote it down has only "until the
  compartments separated there was a disorderly, fast rotation".
- **A spill from the sanitary device.** In spacefacts.de, Encyclopedia Astronautica and Wikipedia,
  as what made the cabin "very unpleasant". It is in neither Kamanin nor Chertok; his report says
  only that he took a laxative on the third day and used the device.
