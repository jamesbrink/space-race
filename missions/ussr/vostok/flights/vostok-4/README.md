# Vostok 4

**12 to 15 August 1962 · Pavel Popovich · callsign Беркут (Berkut, "Golden Eagle") · Vostok 3KA No. 6 · Vostok-K 8K72K No. E103-22 · Site 1, Tyuratam**

The second ship of the first group flight: launched from the pad [Vostok 3](../vostok-3/README.md)
had left 23 hours 32 minutes before, into an orbit that began about five kilometres from it. Three
days and forty-eight orbits; the first conversation between two crewed ships; a cabin that went
cold and cost its pilot a fourth day; ending with the pilot ejecting and landing by parachute six
minutes after Nikolayev. Backup: Vladimir Komarov; reserve for both ships: Boris Volynov.

## What happened

All times are Moscow time (UTC+3). How the pair of flights came about, and the days before the
first launch, are told in [Vostok 3's README](../vostok-3/README.md).

A Vostok could not change its orbit. To have two fly together the second had to leave the pad at
the instant the turning Earth carried it under the first one's track, a few minutes less than a
day after the first, and be shut down at the right speed. OKB-1 wanted it as the first step of a
rendezvous for the ships to come, where the launch alone was to bring two craft within twenty
kilometres.

Ship No. 6 was the difficulty. It went through its full round of tests a third time on 5 August
and had passed them by the 8th; its ejection seat was still on the bench on the 9th when a
rejected washer was found among the spares. On the morning of the 9th Popovich practised leaving
the seat and floating, in his suit. Once Vostok 3 had gone on the 11th the pad was cleared and the
second rocket brought out; the launch crew worked through.

Liftoff was at 11:02:33 on 12 August. Nikolayev, overhead with his ship turned to look, saw
nothing of it. In orbit Popovich looked for Vostok 3 where he had been told it would be and, he
said in Moscow, "saw it at once … something like a very small moon in the distance". The two had
each other on short wave at once. The ships were about five kilometres apart at the start and
drew apart from then: 850 km a day later. TASS's figures at 12:12 gave the orbits as 177.9 by
234.8 km and, for Vostok 3, 176.7 by 227.6 km.

The two kept one timetable. Popovich left his seat and floated each day for about an hour, as
Nikolayev had told him over the radio it could be done. He oriented the ship by hand in the
Earth's shadow. Before the television cameras he was the livelier of the two, letting pencils and
his log book go in front of the lens. He shook a flask two-thirds full of water and watched the
air break into bubbles and gather again. He filmed the horizon and the terminator. He found dust
on a paper napkin and ran the vacuum cleaner over the cabin. Quick nods of the head gave him "some
abnormalities"; his pulse was 54 to 60. He slept at 21:30 on the 12th and was woken at 04:53; at night
he switched his suit's fan off.

On the morning of the 14th both landings were set for the 15th: Vostok 3 after four days, Vostok 4
after three, a day more for each than first planned. That afternoon Smirnov and Korolev spoke to Khrushchev and Kozlov,
and Khrushchev asked why Popovich should be "offended" if he was well and wished to fly on; at
17:00 the State Commission took up a fourth day. Kamanin, Gagarin, Bushuyev and Feoktistov were
against: the landing grounds on the 65th orbit were bad and windy, Popovich was spending himself,
and his cabin was at 11 to 12 °C and falling. Korolev did not see the point but would not vote
against. Asked over the radio what his plans were, Popovich said he meant to land on the 65th
orbit, feeling first rate. It was agreed.

He woke at 04:00 on the 15th and reported that the temperature and humidity were still going
down: "I have taken every measure and the fall continues." By 07:00 the cabin was at 10 °C, the
lowest allowed, and the humidity at 35%. Keldysh, Kamanin and Rudenko wanted him down on the 49th
orbit, which was coming up; Smirnov held out. Then a message was brought in: "I am observing a
thunderstorm." *Гроза* was the agreed code for vomiting. There were forty minutes to decide. Asked
again, he answered that he felt excellent and had seen "a meteorological thunderstorm and
lightning". He landed on the 49th orbit.

The braking engine fired six minutes after Vostok 3's. He ejected smoothly, locked his reserve
parachute once the main was open, and landed at 09:59 south of Karaganda, in a wind he later said
had nearly hurt him. A search aircraft was over him in twenty minutes. The descent module had
landed about thirteen minutes before him.

At the press conference of 21 August he was asked whether he had landed in his ship. "Beside the
ship," he said, like those before him.

## Where the sources disagree

- **The liftoff time.** 11:02:33 (Siddiqi's launch log; spacefacts.de as 08:02:33 UTC), which
  `flight.ts` takes. 08:02:17 UTC in McDowell's launch log. 11:02 in TASS and from Keldysh and
  Nikolayev on 21 August. Kamanin's diary as printed says the time set three days before was
  **11:08** and that it was kept to half a second; but the same diary records Nikolayev reporting
  that he turned his ship fifteen minutes before Popovich's launch and had it in place at 11:03,
  which fits 11:02 and not 11:08. RussianSpaceWeb follows the 11:08. It is read here as a slip in
  the diary or its printing.
- **The landing time.** 09:59 (the duration in Siddiqi's table, 2 d 22 h 56 min 27 s; Russian
  Wikipedia; spacefacts.de) or 10:01 (the TASS message of 15 August as *Flight* printed it).
  Siddiqi's text has 2 d 22 h 57 min. Kamanin, Chertok and Siddiqi all have him down six minutes
  after Nikolayev; 09:52 and 09:59 are seven apart, and TASS's 09:55 and 10:01 six.
- **How far apart they landed.** "About 200 km" (Popovich, 21 August; Siddiqi). The places as
  printed (48°09′ N 71°51′ E and 48°02′ N 75°45′ E) are about 290 km apart, and RussianSpaceWeb
  has 305 km.
- **How near the ships came, and whether he saw Vostok 3.** As for Vostok 3: five kilometres or
  six and a half; "I saw it at once" against Siddiqi's "it seems … neither cosmonaut ever spotted
  the other's spacecraft".
- **What he said about landing.** *Flight*'s text of the press conference: "Like Herman Titov and
  Andrian Grigoryevich, I landed beside the ship." Siddiqi (p. 360, note 30, from a US Senate
  report): "Like Titov and Gagarin, I landed beside the ship", which he reads as a slip from the
  official line that Gagarin had landed in his ship.
- **His pulse in the climb.** 102 to 104 (TASS, 12 August); "up to 130" (Yazdovsky, 21 August).
- **The landing as he told it.** Uneventful in the report Kamanin wrote down in 1962; a fight with
  the wind and with the parachutes of rescue doctors who should not have jumped, in an interview
  decades later (*Novosti Kosmonavtiki*, as RussianSpaceWeb gives it; not read).
- **The launch vehicle's number.** E103-22 in McDowell's launch log and on spacefacts.de, with
  E103-23 for Vostok 3. Siddiqi's launch log gives no number.
- **His age.** Siddiqi calls him "also thirty-two"; no document giving his date of birth was read
  for this flight, so `flight.ts` gives no age.
